> **Theorem 7.1.1 (Vertical Angles Are Equal)**
> 
> Vertical angles are equal.

> **Theorem 7.1.2 (The Perpendicular Segment Is the Shortest)**
> 
> Among all segments joining a point outside a line to points on the line, the perpendicular segment is the shortest. Stated briefly: the perpendicular segment is the shortest.

> **Definition 7.1.3 (Perpendicular)**
> 
> When one of the four angles formed by two intersecting lines is a right angle, the two lines are said to be perpendicular to each other, written $a\perp{b}$. One of the lines is called the perpendicular line of the other, and the intersection is called the foot of the perpendicular.

> **Definition 7.2.1 (Parallel)**
> 
> In the same plane, two lines that do not intersect are called parallel lines, denoted $a\parallel{b}$. In the same plane, two distinct lines have only two possible positional relationships: intersecting and parallel.

> **Basic Fact 7.2.1 (Parallel Axiom)**
> 
> Through a point outside a given line there is exactly one line parallel to the given line.

> **Theorem 7.2.1 (Transitivity of Parallelism)**
> 
> If two lines are both parallel to a third line, then these two lines are also parallel to each other. That is, if $b\parallel{a}$, $c\parallel{a}$, then $b\parallel{c}$.

> **Property 7.2.1 (Property 1)**
> 
> When two parallel lines are cut by a third line, the corresponding angles are equal. In short: parallel lines give equal corresponding angles.

> **Basic Fact 7.2.2 (Criterion 1)**
> 
> If two lines are cut by a third line and the corresponding angles are equal, then the two lines are parallel. In short: equal corresponding angles imply parallel lines.

> **Theorem 7.2.2 (Criterion 2)**
> 
> If two lines are cut by a third line and the alternate interior angles are equal, then the two lines are parallel. In short: equal alternate interior angles imply parallel lines.

> **Property 7.2.2 (Property 2)**
> 
> When two parallel lines are cut by a third line, the alternate interior angles are equal. In short: parallel lines give equal alternate interior angles.

> **Theorem 7.2.3 (Criterion 3)**
> 
> If two lines are cut by a third line and the consecutive interior angles are supplementary, then the two lines are parallel. In short: supplementary consecutive interior angles imply parallel lines.

> **Property 7.2.3 (Property 3)**
> 
> When two parallel lines are cut by a third line, the consecutive interior angles are supplementary. In short: parallel lines give supplementary consecutive interior angles.

> **Definition 7.3.1 (Definition)**
> 
> A clear, explicit description of a mathematical object that reveals its essential features is called a definition of the mathematical object.

> **Method 7.3.1 (Giving a Counterexample)**
> 
> To judge that a proposition is false, it suffices to give a counterexample: one that satisfies the hypothesis of the proposition but does not satisfy the conclusion.

> **Definition 7.3.2 (Proposition)**
> 
> A declarative statement that can be judged to be true (correct) or false (incorrect) is called a proposition. A proposition judged true is called a true proposition, and one judged false is called a false proposition. A proposition is often written in the form "If …, then …"; after "if" comes the hypothesis, and after "then" comes the conclusion.

> **Definition 7.3.3 (Theorem)**
> 
> A true proposition whose correctness has been confirmed through reasoning is called a theorem. A theorem can also serve as a basis for further reasoning.

> **Definition 7.3.4 (Proof)**
> 
> When the correctness of a proposition can be judged only through reasoning, this process of reasoning is called a proof.

> **Definition 7.4.1 (Translation)**
> 
> In a plane, moving a figure by a certain distance in a certain direction is called a translation of the figure. The direction of translation is not limited to horizontal or vertical; it may be along any direction in the plane.

> **Property 7.4.1 (Properties of Translation)**
> 
> After translation, the new figure has exactly the same shape and size as the original figure; the segments connecting each pair of corresponding points are parallel (or lie on the same line) and equal.

## Problem 6

(Exercise 7.2, Extension and Exploration, Problem 14) As shown in the figure, the two mirrors in a periscope are placed parallel to each other. When light rays reflect off the mirrors, $\angle1=\angle2$, $\angle3=\angle4$. What is the relationship between $\angle2$ and $\angle3$? Why is the ray entering the periscope parallel to the ray leaving the periscope?

The figure shows a cross-section of a periscope: two mirrors placed parallel to each other, one above the other; an incoming ray enters from one side, reflects off the first mirror, strikes the second mirror, and reflects out the other side. At the first mirror are marked $\angle1$ (angle between incident ray and mirror) and $\angle2$ (angle between reflected ray and mirror); at the second mirror are marked the corresponding $\angle3$ and $\angle4$.

### Solution to Problem 6

The two mirrors are parallel to each other. The two reflected rays between them are cut by the mirrors (lines); $\angle2$ and $\angle3$ are the alternate interior angles formed when the two parallel mirrors are cut by the middle ray. By Property 2, parallel lines give equal alternate interior angles, so $\angle2=\angle3$.

Also given $\angle1=\angle2$, $\angle3=\angle4$, so $\angle1=\angle2=\angle3=\angle4$. Thus the alternate interior angles formed when the ray entering the periscope and the ray leaving the periscope are cut by the reflected ray between the mirrors

$$
\angle1+\angle2=\angle3+\angle4
$$

hold. By **Theorem 7.2.2 (Criterion 2)**, equal alternate interior angles imply parallel lines, so the ray entering the periscope is parallel to the ray leaving the periscope. $\blacksquare$

## Problem 7

(Exercise 7.3, Review and Consolidation, Problem 1) Which of the following statements are propositions? Which are true propositions?

(1) If $a=b$, $b=c$, then $a=c$;

(2) The supplementary angles of equal angles are equal;

(3) Through a point draw the perpendicular to line $l$;

(4) The sum of two acute angles is an obtuse angle.

### Solution to Problem 7

By **Definition 7.3.2 (Proposition)**, a proposition is a declarative statement that can be judged true or false.

(1) "If $a=b$, $b=c$, then $a=c$" is a declarative statement that can be judged true or false; it is a proposition, and by transitivity of equality it is true, so it is a true proposition.

(2) "The supplementary angles of equal angles are equal" is a declarative statement that can be judged true or false; it is a proposition, and the supplementary angles of equal angles are indeed equal, so it is a true proposition.

(3) "Through a point draw the perpendicular to line $l$" is merely an operational instruction and cannot be judged true or false, so it is not a proposition.

(4) "The sum of two acute angles is an obtuse angle" is a declarative statement that can be judged true or false; it is a proposition. But the sum of the two acute angles $30^\circ$ and $30^\circ$ is $60^\circ$, which is still an acute angle, so by Giving a Counterexample, it is a false proposition.

In summary, (1)(2)(4) are propositions, among which (1)(2) are true propositions. $\blacksquare$

## Exercise 6

(Exercise 7.4, Problem 1) On grid paper, translate triangle $ABC$ so that point $A$ moves to point $M$. To what positions should points $B$ and $C$ move? Then translate the triangle again so that point $A$ moves from point $M$ to point $N$. Draw the triangles after the two translations respectively. If triangle $ABC$ is translated directly so that point $A$ moves to point $N$, is the translated triangle in the same position as the triangle obtained after the second translation above?

The figure shows grid paper with triangle $ABC$ drawn on it, and points $M$ and $N$ marked.

### Solution to Exercise 6

By Properties of Translation, during translation every point on the figure moves the same distance in the same direction, and the segments connecting each pair of corresponding points are parallel and equal.

Point $A$ moves to point $M$, i.e. translation by the direction and distance of $\boldsymbol{AM}$. Thus point $B$ moves the same distance in the same direction as $\boldsymbol{AM}$ to its corresponding point, and point $C$ likewise. Connecting the three points gives the triangle after the first translation.

Then move point $A$ (now at $M$) to point $N$, i.e. translate once more by the direction and distance of $\boldsymbol{MN}$, giving the triangle after the second translation.

Translating triangle $ABC$ directly so that point $A$ moves to point $N$ has translation vector $\boldsymbol{AN}$. By vector addition, $\boldsymbol{AN}=\boldsymbol{AM}+\boldsymbol{MN}$, i.e. the combined effect of first translating by $\boldsymbol{AM}$ and then by $\boldsymbol{MN}$ is the same as translating directly by $\boldsymbol{AN}$. Thus the triangle obtained by direct translation is in the same position as the triangle obtained after the second translation. $\blacksquare$

## Problem 14

(Exercise 7.4, Comprehensive Application, Problem 4) Use the method of translation to explain how to obtain the area formula of a parallelogram, $S=ah$.

The figure shows a parallelogram with base $a$ and height $h$; a right triangle is cut from one side by dropping a height to the base, and the diagram illustrates translating this right triangle along the base to the other side to form a rectangle of length $a$ and width $h$.

### Solution to Problem 14

By Properties of Translation, translation does not change the shape or size of a figure.

As shown in the figure, let the base of the parallelogram be $a$ and the height be $h$. Cut off a right triangle from one side of the parallelogram and translate it along the direction of the base to the other side; it exactly fills in to form a rectangle. This rectangle has length $a$ and width $h$.

Since translation does not change the area of the figure, the area of the parallelogram equals the area of the rectangle formed, i.e.

$$
S=ah.
$$

Thus the area formula of the parallelogram is $S=ah$. $\blacksquare$

## Problem 16

(Exercise 7.4, Extension and Exploration, Problem 6) As shown in the figure, on a rectangular lawn of length $a\,\text{m}$ and width $b\,\text{m}$, there is a winding path; the left edge of the path, translated $1\,\text{m}$ to the right, is its right edge. Find the area covered by grass on this lawn.

The figure shows a rectangular lawn of length $a$ and width $b$, with a winding path running through it; the left edge of the path, translated $1\,\text{m}$ to the right, is its right edge.

### Solution to Problem 16

By Properties of Translation, translation does not change the shape or size of a figure.

The left edge of the path translated $1\,\text{m}$ to the right is the right edge, which means the path has width $1\,\text{m}$ in any transverse direction. Translate the lawn on both sides of the path toward the middle and join them; they form a new rectangle whose width is still $b$ and whose length is reduced by $1$, namely $a-1$.

Since translation does not change area, the grass-covered area equals the area of the joined rectangle:

$$
S=\left(a-1\right)b=ab-b\quad\left(\text{m}^2\right).
$$

Thus the grass-covered area of this lawn is $\left(ab-b\right)\,\text{m}^2$. $\blacksquare$

## Activity 1

(Mathematical Activity, Activity 1) How many ways do you have to draw parallel lines? After learning about parallel lines, three students—Li Ming, Liu Wei, and Wang Fang—each came up with a new method for drawing a line through a point outside a given line that is parallel to it.

Liu Wei's method: as shown in the figure, draw $PQ\perp{a}$, then draw $l\perp{a}$, take $RS=PQ$ on $l$, and connect $PS$; then $PS\parallel{a}$.

### Solution to Activity 1

Wang Fang folds the paper to create two crease lines that coincide with each other; after unfolding, the two crease lines do not intersect in the same plane, so they are parallel lines, and the method is correct.

## Review Exercise 1

(Review Exercise, Review and Consolidation, Problem 1) As shown in the figure, line $AB\perp{C}D$, with foot at $O$; line $EF$ passes through point $O$, $\angle1=26^\circ$. Find the degrees of $\angle2$, $\angle3$, and $\angle{B}OE$.

The figure shows lines $AB\perp{C}D$ with foot $O$, and a line $EF$ passing through $O$; inside $\angle{A}OD$ are marked $\angle1$ (between $OE$ and $OD$) and $\angle2$ (between $OA$ and $OE$), and the vertical angle of $\angle1$ is marked as $\angle3$.

### Solution to Review Exercise 1

By **Definition 7.1.3 (Perpendicular)**, $AB\perp{C}D$, so $\angle{A}OD=90^\circ$.

$\angle1$ and $\angle2$ lie inside the right angle $\angle{A}OD$, and $\angle{A}OD=\angle1+\angle2$, so $\angle2=90^\circ-\angle1=90^\circ-26^\circ=64^\circ$.

$\angle3$ and $\angle1$ are vertical angles; by **Theorem 7.1.1 (Vertical Angles Are Equal)**, $\angle3=\angle1=26^\circ$.

$\angle{B}OE$ and $\angle1$ share the side $OE$, while the other sides $OB$ and $OA$ are opposite extensions of each other; together they form a straight angle, so $\angle{B}OE=180^\circ-\angle1=180^\circ-26^\circ=154^\circ$.

Thus $\angle2=64^\circ$, $\angle3=26^\circ$, $\angle{B}OE=154^\circ$. $\blacksquare$
