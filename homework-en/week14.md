> **Definition 6.1.1 (Solid Figure)**
> 
> A geometric figure whose parts do not all lie in the same plane is called a solid figure.

> **Definition 6.1.2 (Plane Figure)**
> 
> A geometric figure whose parts all lie in the same plane is called a plane figure.

> **Definition 6.1.3 (Net)**
> 
> Some solid figures are enclosed by several plane figures; if their surfaces are cut open appropriately, they can be unfolded into plane figures. Such a plane figure is called the net of the corresponding solid figure.

> **Definition 6.1.4 (Points, Lines, Planes, and Solids)**
> 
> A geometric solid is also called a solid for short. What encloses a solid is a surface; where two surfaces intersect forms a line; where two lines intersect is a point.

> **Definition 6.1.5 (A Moving Point Forms a Line, a Moving Line Forms a Plane, a Moving Plane Forms a Solid)**
> 
> A moving point forms a line, a moving line forms a plane, and a moving plane forms a solid.

> **Definition 6.2.1 (Line)**
> 
> Through two points there is one line, and only one line. In short: two points determine a line.

> **Definition 6.2.3 (Ray and Segment)**
> 
> A point on a line and the part on one side of it is called a ray, and this point is called the endpoint of the ray. Two points on a line and the part between them is called a segment, and these two points are called the endpoints of the segment.

> **Definition 6.2.5 (Basic Fact about Segments)**
> 
> Between two points, the segment is the shortest.

> **Definition 6.2.6 (Distance)**
> 
> The length of the segment joining two points is called the distance between them.

> **Definition 6.2.7 (Midpoint of a Segment)**
> 
> Point $M$ divides segment $AB$ into two equal segments $AM$ and $MB$; point $M$ is called the midpoint of segment $AB$.

> **Definition 6.3.1 (Angle)**
> 
> The figure formed by two rays with a common endpoint is called an angle.

> **Definition 6.3.2 (Measuring Angles)**
> 
> $1^{\circ}=60'$, $1'=60''$. A full angle $=360^{\circ}$.

> **Definition 6.3.3 (Comparing and Operating on Angles)**
> 
> To compare angles, one may measure their degrees with a protractor, or overlay the two angles. Addition and subtraction of angles are carried out based on their degrees.

> **Definition 6.3.4 (Angle Bisector)**
> 
> A ray starting from the vertex of an angle that divides the angle into two equal angles is called the angle bisector.

> **Definition 6.3.5 (Complementary Angle)**
> 
> If the sum of two angles equals $90^{\circ}$ (a right angle), we say the two angles are complementary to each other; that is, each angle is the complement of the other.

> **Definition 6.3.6 (Supplementary Angle)**
> 
> If the sum of two angles equals $180^{\circ}$ (a straight angle), we say the two angles are supplementary to each other; that is, each angle is the supplement of the other.

> **Definition 6.3.7 (Properties of Complementary and Supplementary Angles)**
> 
> Complements of the same angle (or of equal angles) are equal; supplements of the same angle (or of equal angles) are equal.

## Exercise 1

(Exercise 6.3, Problem 1) As shown, $\angle{A}OC=40^{\circ}$, $\angle{C}OE=25^{\circ}$, and $OD$ is the bisector of $\angle{A}OB$; find the degree measure of $\angle{D}OB$.

### Solution to Exercise 1

By **Definition 6.3.3 (Comparing and Operating on Angles)** and **Definition 6.3.4 (Angle Bisector)**.

$$\angle{A}OE=\angle{A}OC+\angle{C}OE=40^{\circ}+25^{\circ}=65^{\circ}.$$

If $\angle{A}OB=\angle{A}OE=65^{\circ}$, and $OD$ bisects $\angle{A}OB$, then

$$\angle{D}OB=\frac{1}{2}\angle{A}OB=\frac{1}{2}\times65^{\circ}=32.5^{\circ}.$$

$\blacksquare$

## Exercise 3

(Exercise 6.3, Problem 3) As shown, given $\angle{A}OB=60^{\circ}$, $\angle{B}OC=40^{\circ}$, $OM$ bisects $\angle{A}OC$, and $ON$ bisects $\angle{B}OC$; find the degree measure of $\angle{M}ON$.

### Solution to Exercise 3

By **Definition 6.3.4 (Angle Bisector)**.

$$\angle{A}OC=\angle{A}OB+\angle{B}OC=60^{\circ}+40^{\circ}=100^{\circ}.$$

$OM$ bisects $\angle{A}OC$, so $\angle{M}OC=\frac{1}{2}\angle{A}OC=50^{\circ}$.

$ON$ bisects $\angle{B}OC$, so $\angle{N}OC=\frac{1}{2}\angle{B}OC=20^{\circ}$.

$$\angle{M}ON=\angle{M}OC-\angle{N}OC=50^{\circ}-20^{\circ}=30^{\circ}.$$

$\blacksquare$

## Exercise 4

(Exercise 6.3, Problem 1) As shown, the right-angle vertices of two set squares coincide at point $O$; find the degree measure of $\angle{A}OD+\angle{B}OC$.

### Solution to Exercise 4

By **Definition 6.3.3 (Comparing and Operating on Angles)** and **Definition 6.3.5 (Complementary Angle)**.

The right-angle vertices of the two set squares coincide, i.e. $\angle{A}OB=\angle{C}OD=90^{\circ}$.

$$\angle{A}OD=\angle{A}OB+\angle{B}OD=90^{\circ}+\angle{B}OD.$$

$$\angle{B}OC=\angle{C}OD-\angle{B}OD=90^{\circ}-\angle{B}OD.$$

$$\angle{A}OD+\angle{B}OC=\left(90^{\circ}+\angle{B}OD\right)+\left(90^{\circ}-\angle{B}OD\right)=180^{\circ}.$$

$\blacksquare$

## Exercise 5

(Exercise 6.3, Problem 2) The complement of an angle is $20^{\circ}$ less than $\frac{1}{3}$ of its supplement; find the degree measure of the angle.

### Solution to Exercise 5

By **Definition 6.3.5 (Complementary Angle)** and **Definition 6.3.6 (Supplementary Angle)**, let the angle be $x^{\circ}$.

Its complement is $\left(90-x\right)^{\circ}$, and its supplement is $\left(180-x\right)^{\circ}$.

$$90-x=\frac{1}{3}\left(180-x\right)-20.$$

Simplifying: $90-x=60-\frac{x}{3}-20$. $90-x=40-\frac{x}{3}$. $50=\frac{2x}{3}$. $x=75$. Hence the angle is $75^{\circ}$. $\blacksquare$

## Exercise 6

(Exercise 6.3, Problem 3) As shown, point $O$ lies on line $AB$, $OD$ bisects $\angle{A}OC$, and $OE$ bisects $\angle{B}OC$; find the degree measure of $\angle{D}OE$.

### Solution to Exercise 6

By **Definition 6.3.4 (Angle Bisector)** and **Definition 6.3.6 (Supplementary Angle)**.

$O$ lies on line $AB$, so $\angle{A}OC+\angle{B}OC=180^{\circ}$.

$OD$ bisects $\angle{A}OC$, so $\angle{D}OC=\frac{1}{2}\angle{A}OC$.

$OE$ bisects $\angle{B}OC$, so $\angle{C}OE=\frac{1}{2}\angle{B}OC$.

$$\angle{D}OE=\angle{D}OC+\angle{C}OE=\frac{1}{2}\left(\angle{A}OC+\angle{B}OC\right)=\frac{1}{2}\times180^{\circ}=90^{\circ}.$$

$\blacksquare$

## Exercise 7

(Exercise 6.3, Problem 4) The supplement of an angle is $4$ times its complement; find the degree measure of the angle.

### Solution to Exercise 7

By **Definition 6.3.5 (Complementary Angle)** and **Definition 6.3.6 (Supplementary Angle)**, let the angle be $x^{\circ}$.

$$180-x=4\left(90-x\right).$$

Simplifying: $180-x=360-4x$. $3x=180$. $x=60$. Hence the angle is $60^{\circ}$. $\blacksquare$

## Problem 10

(Problem 6.3, Comprehensive Application, Problem 10) As shown, $OB$ is the bisector of $\angle{A}OC$, and $OD$ is the bisector of $\angle{C}OE$.

(1) If $\angle{A}OB=40^{\circ}$ and $\angle{D}OE=30^{\circ}$, find the degree measure of $\angle{B}OD$;

(2) If $\angle{A}OE=140^{\circ}$ and $\angle{C}OD=30^{\circ}$, find the degree measure of $\angle{A}OB$.

### Solution to Problem 10

By **Definition 6.3.4 (Angle Bisector)**.

(1) $OB$ bisects $\angle{A}OC$, so $\angle{B}OC=\angle{A}OB=40^{\circ}$. $OD$ bisects $\angle{C}OE$, so $\angle{C}OD=\angle{D}OE=30^{\circ}$.

$$\angle{B}OD=\angle{B}OC+\angle{C}OD=40^{\circ}+30^{\circ}=70^{\circ}.$$

(2) $OD$ bisects $\angle{C}OE$, so $\angle{C}OE=2\angle{C}OD=60^{\circ}$.

$$\angle{A}OC=\angle{A}OE-\angle{C}OE=140^{\circ}-60^{\circ}=80^{\circ}.$$

$OB$ bisects $\angle{A}OC$, so $\angle{A}OB=\frac{1}{2}\angle{A}OC=40^{\circ}$. $\blacksquare$

## Problem 11

(Problem 6.3, Comprehensive Application, Problem 11) As shown, given $\angle{A}OB=90^{\circ}$, $\angle{B}OC=60^{\circ}$, $OM$ bisects $\angle{A}OC$, and $ON$ bisects $\angle{B}OC$.

(1) Find the degree measure of $\angle{M}ON$;

(2) If $\angle{A}OB=\alpha$, with other conditions unchanged, find the degree measure of $\angle{M}ON$.

### Solution to Problem 11

By **Definition 6.3.4 (Angle Bisector)**.

(1) $\angle{A}OC=\angle{A}OB+\angle{B}OC=90^{\circ}+60^{\circ}=150^{\circ}$.

$OM$ bisects $\angle{A}OC$, so $\angle{M}OC=\frac{1}{2}\angle{A}OC=75^{\circ}$.

$ON$ bisects $\angle{B}OC$, so $\angle{N}OC=\frac{1}{2}\angle{B}OC=30^{\circ}$.

$$\angle{M}ON=\angle{M}OC-\angle{N}OC=75^{\circ}-30^{\circ}=45^{\circ}.$$

(2) If $\angle{A}OB=\alpha$, then $\angle{A}OC=\alpha+60^{\circ}$.

$$\angle{M}OC=\frac{1}{2}\left(\alpha+60^{\circ}\right),\quad\angle{N}OC=\frac{1}{2}\times60^{\circ}=30^{\circ}.$$

$$\angle{M}ON=\frac{1}{2}\left(\alpha+60^{\circ}\right)-30^{\circ}=\frac{\alpha}{2}.$$

$\blacksquare$

## Problem 15

(Problem 6.3, Extension and Exploration, Problem 15) As shown, $6$ rays $OA$, $OB$, $OC$, $OD$, $OE$, $OF$ emanate from point $O$, with $\angle{A}OB=100^{\circ}$, $\angle{F}OD=40^{\circ}$, $\angle{E}OF=30^{\circ}$, $OG$ bisects $\angle{C}OE$, and $\angle{C}OG=30^{\circ}$. Find the degree measure of $\angle{B}OC$.

### Solution to Problem 15

By **Definition 6.3.3 (Comparing and Operating on Angles)** and **Definition 6.3.4 (Angle Bisector)**.

By **Definition 6.3.2 (Measuring Angles)**, figure (1) shows the three exterior angles $\angle1$, $\angle2$, $\angle3$ of triangle $ABC$; figure (2) shows the four exterior angles $\angle1$, $\angle2$, $\angle3$, $\angle4$ of a quadrilateral.

(1) Each exterior angle of a triangle is supplementary to its adjacent interior angle. The sum of the three exterior angles $=3\times180^{\circ}-$ the sum of the three interior angles $=540^{\circ}-180^{\circ}=360^{\circ}$. That is,

$$\angle1+\angle2+\angle3=360^{\circ}.$$

(2) Each exterior angle of a quadrilateral is supplementary to its adjacent interior angle. The sum of the four exterior angles $=4\times180^{\circ}-$ the sum of the four interior angles $=720^{\circ}-360^{\circ}=360^{\circ}$. That is,

$$\angle1+\angle2+\angle3+\angle4=360^{\circ}.$$

(3) Combining (1) and (2), we may conjecture: the sum of the exterior angles of any polygon is always $360^{\circ}$. $\blacksquare$

## Reading and Thinking 1

(Reading and Thinking: Measuring Angles) How did the degree system arise? What is radian measure?

### Solution to Reading and Thinking 1

By **Definition 6.3.2 (Measuring Angles)**, the degree system originated from the ancient Babylonians' practice of dividing a circle into $360$ parts.

The degree system uses degrees, minutes, and seconds as units, $1^{\circ}=60'$, $1'=60''$, and is base $60$. Radian measure measures an angle by the ratio of arc length to radius; $1$ radian is the central angle subtended by an arc equal in length to the radius, and a full angle $=2\pi$ radians. $\blacksquare$

## Activity 3

(Mathematical Activity, Activity 2, Problem 1) Follow the steps to draw a five-pointed star:

(1) Draw any circle;

(2) With the center as vertex, successively draw angles of $72^{\circ}$, intersecting the circle at five points;

(3) Connect every other pair of points;

(4) Erase the extra lines to obtain a five-pointed star.

Explain the reasoning behind this construction.

### Solution to Activity 3

By **Definition 6.3.1 (Angle)** and **Definition 6.3.2 (Measuring Angles)**.

A full angle is $360^{\circ}$; dividing it into $5$ equal parts gives $72^{\circ}$ each. Successively drawing $72^{\circ}$ angles with the center as vertex gives five equally spaced points on the circle, namely the five vertices of the inscribed regular pentagon.

Connecting every other pair of points means drawing the diagonals of the regular pentagon; the five diagonals intersect to form a five-pointed star. This is because the diagonals of a regular pentagon exactly form a regular pentagram, with each sharp tip $36^{\circ}$.

The figure shows a circle with its inscribed regular pentagon: five equally spaced points on the circle (adjacent points separated by $72^{\circ}$) are arranged in order, and connecting every other pair of points (the five diagonals of the regular pentagon) yields a five-pointed star.

$\blacksquare$
