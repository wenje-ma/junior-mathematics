> **Theorem 29.2.1 (Perpendicular Chord Theorem)**
> 
> A diameter perpendicular to a chord bisects the chord and bisects the two arcs subtended by the chord.

> **Definition 29.2.1 (Central Angle)**
> 
> An angle whose vertex is at the center of a circle is called a central angle.

> **Property 29.2.2 (Rotational Symmetry of a Circle)**
> 
> A circle is a centrally symmetric figure, and its center is its center of symmetry; rotating a circle about its center through any angle gives a figure that coincides with the original.

> **Theorem 29.2.2 (Relationship Among Central Angles, Arcs, and Chords)**
> 
> In the same circle or equal circles, equal central angles subtend equal arcs and equal chords; equal arcs subtend equal central angles and equal chords; equal chords subtend equal central angles, and their major arcs and minor arcs are respectively equal.

> **Definition 29.2.2 (Inscribed Angle)**
> 
> An angle whose vertex lies on a circle and whose both sides intersect the circle is called an inscribed angle.

> **Corollary 29.2.2 (Corollary to the Inscribed Angle Theorem)**
> 
> Inscribed angles subtended by the same arc or equal arcs are equal; the inscribed angle subtended by a semicircle (or a diameter) is a right angle; the chord subtended by an inscribed angle of $90^\circ$ is a diameter.

> **Theorem 29.2.3 (Inscribed Angle Theorem)**
> 
> The inscribed angle subtended by an arc is half of the central angle subtended by that arc.

> **Definition 29.2.3 (Polygon Inscribed in a Circle)**
> 
> If all vertices of a polygon lie on the same circle, the polygon is called a polygon inscribed in the circle, and the circle is called the circumcircle of the polygon.

> **Property 29.2.3 (Property of a Cyclic Quadrilateral)**
> 
> The opposite angles of a cyclic quadrilateral are supplementary.

> **Formula 29.3.1 (Arc Length Formula)**
> 
> In a circle of radius $R$, the arc length subtended by a central angle of $n^\circ$ is $l=\frac{n\pi{R}}{180}$.

> **Definition 29.3.1 (Generatrix of a Cone)**
> 
> The segment joining the vertex of a cone to any point on the circumference of its base is called the generatrix of the cone.

> **Formula 29.3.2 (Sector Area Formula)**
> 
> In a circle of radius $R$, the area of a sector with central angle $n^\circ$ is $S=\frac{n\pi{R}^2}{360}=\frac12lR$, where $l$ is the arc length of the sector.

> **Formula 29.3.3 (Lateral and Total Surface Area of a Cone)**
> 
> If the generatrix length of a cone is $l$ and the base radius is $r$, then the lateral area is $\pi{r}l$ and the total surface area is $\pi{r}\left(r+l\right)$.

## Exercise 1

(Exercise 29.2, Problem 1) As shown in the figure, $AB$ and $CD$ are two chords of $\odot{O}$.

(1) If $AB=CD$, then $\_\_\_$;

(2) If $\overset{\frown}{AB}=\overset{\frown}{CD}$, then $\_\_\_$;

(3) If $\angle{A}OB=\angle{C}OD$, then $\_\_\_$;

(4) If $AB=CD$, $OE\perp{A}B$, $OF\perp{C}D$, with feet $E$ and $F$ respectively, are $OE$ and $OF$ equal? Why?

### Solution to Exercise 1

By **Theorem 29.2.2 (Relationship Among Central Angles, Arcs, and Chords)**.

(1) $\angle{A}OB=\angle{C}OD$, $\overset{\frown}{AB}=\overset{\frown}{CD}$.

(2) $AB=CD$, $\angle{A}OB=\angle{C}OD$.

(3) $AB=CD$, $\overset{\frown}{AB}=\overset{\frown}{CD}$.

(4) $OE=OF$. Since $AB=CD$, in $\triangle{O}AB$ and $\triangle{O}CD$ we have $OA=OB=OC=OD$; the two triangles are congruent, and $OE$ and $OF$ are the altitudes on corresponding sides, so $OE=OF$. $\blacksquare$

## Exercise 2

(Exercise 29.2, Problem 2) As shown in the figure, $AB$ is a diameter of $\odot{O}$, $\overset{\frown}{BC}=\overset{\frown}{CD}=\overset{\frown}{DE}$, and $\angle{C}OD=35^\circ$. Find the measure of $\angle{A}OE$.

### Solution to Exercise 2

Since $\overset{\frown}{BC}=\overset{\frown}{CD}=\overset{\frown}{DE}$, we have $\angle{B}OC=\angle{C}OD=\angle{D}OE=35^\circ$. The three central angles total $3\times35^\circ=105^\circ$. Since $AB$ is a diameter, $\angle{A}OB=180^\circ$, hence

### Solution to Exercise 3

Since $\overset{\frown}{AC}=\overset{\frown}{BC}$, we have $\angle{A}OC=\angle{B}OC$. Since $M$, $N$ are the midpoints of $OA$, $OB$ respectively, $OM=\frac12OA=\frac12OB=ON$. In $\triangle{O}MC$ and $\triangle{O}NC$, $OM=ON$, $\angle{M}OC=\angle{N}OC$, and $OC$ is common, so $\triangle{O}MC\cong\triangle{O}NC$, and hence $MC=NC$. $\blacksquare$

## Exercise 5

(Exercise 29.2, Problem 2) As shown in the figure, $OA$, $OB$, $OC$ are all radii of $\odot{O}$, and $\angle{A}OB=2\angle{B}OC$. Prove that $\angle{A}CB=2\angle{B}AC$.

### Solution to Exercise 5

By **Theorem 29.2.3 (Inscribed Angle Theorem)**.

$\angle{A}CB$ is the inscribed angle subtended by $\overset{\frown}{AB}$, so $\angle{A}CB=\frac12\angle{A}OB$. $\angle{B}AC$ is the inscribed angle subtended by $\overset{\frown}{BC}$, so $\angle{B}AC=\frac12\angle{B}OC$. From $\angle{A}OB=2\angle{B}OC$, we get

$$
\angle{A}CB=\frac12\cdot2\angle{B}OC=2\cdot\frac12\angle{B}OC=2\angle{B}AC.
$$

$\blacksquare$

## Exercise 6

(Exercise 29.2, Problem 3) As shown in the figure, can you use a set square to determine the center of a circular sheet of paper? How many methods are there?

### Solution to Exercise 6

By **Corollary 29.2.2 (Corollary to the Inscribed Angle Theorem)**.

Method 1: With the right-angle vertex of the set square at two different points on the circle, draw two chords whose right-angle sides intersect the circle; the chords subtended by the two right-angle sides are diameters, and the intersection of the two diameters is the center.

Method 2: Place the right-angle vertex of the set square on the circle; the two right-angle sides intersect the circle at two points, and joining these two points gives a diameter. Move to another position and draw another diameter; the intersection is the center. $\blacksquare$

## Exercise 7

(Exercise 29.2, Problem 1) As shown in the figure, the diagonals $AC$ and $BD$ of the cyclic quadrilateral $ABCD$ divide its four interior angles into eight angles. Which of these eight angles are equal? Why?

### Solution to Exercise 7

By **Theorem 29.2.3 (Inscribed Angle Theorem)**.

Inscribed angles subtended by the same arc are equal. $\angle{B}AC$ and $\angle{B}DC$ both subtend $\overset{\frown}{BC}$, so they are equal; $\angle{C}BD$ and $\angle{C}AD$ both subtend $\overset{\frown}{CD}$, so they are equal; $\angle{A}CD$ and $\angle{A}BD$ both subtend $\overset{\frown}{AD}$, so they are equal; $\angle{A}DB$ and $\angle{A}CB$ both subtend $\overset{\frown}{AB}$, so they are equal. $\blacksquare$

## Exercise 8

(Exercise 29.2, Problem 2) As shown in the figure, the quadrilateral $ABCD$ is a quadrilateral inscribed in $\odot{O}$, and $E$ is a point on the extension of $CB$. If $\angle{D}=110^\circ$, find the measure of $\angle{A}BE$.

### Solution to Exercise 8

By Property of a Cyclic Quadrilateral.

The opposite angles of a cyclic quadrilateral are supplementary, so $\angle{A}BC+\angle{D}=180^\circ$, hence $\angle{A}BC=180^\circ-110^\circ=70^\circ$. Also $E$ lies on the extension of $CB$, so $\angle{A}BE+\angle{A}BC=180^\circ$, hence $\angle{A}BE=110^\circ$. $\blacksquare$

## Problem 5

(Exercise 29.2, Review and Consolidation, Problem 5) Prove that a parallelogram inscribed in a circle is a rectangle.

### Solution to Problem 5

By Property of a Cyclic Quadrilateral.

The opposite angles of a parallelogram are equal, and the opposite angles of a cyclic quadrilateral are supplementary, so each pair of opposite angles is $90^\circ$, and hence it is a rectangle. $\blacksquare$

## Problem 8

(Exercise 29.2, Comprehensive Application, Problem 8) As shown in the figure, a bend in a road is an arc $\overset{\frown}{AB}$, and the point $O$ is the center of the circle containing this arc. $AB=300\,\text{m}$, $C$ is a point on $\overset{\frown}{AB}$, $OC\perp{A}B$ with foot $D$, and $CD=45\,\text{m}$. ### Solution to Problem 8

By **Theorem 29.2.1 (Perpendicular Chord Theorem)**.

Let the radius be $R$; then $AD=150$ and $OD=R-45$. In $\text{Rt}\triangle{O}AD$,

$$
R^2=150^2+\left(R-45\right)^2=22500+R^2-90R+2025.

Simplifying gives $90R=24525$, so $R=272.5\,\text{m}$. $\blacksquare$

## Problem 12

(Exercise 29.2, Comprehensive Application, Problem 12) As shown in the figure, $AB$ and $CD$ are two chords on $\odot{O}$, with $OM\perp{A}B$, $ON\perp{C}D$, and feet $M$ and $N$ respectively. If $AB>{}CD$, what is the relationship between $OM$ and $ON$? Why?

### Solution to Problem 12

Let the radius be $R$; then $OM=\sqrt{R^2-\left(AB/2\right)^2}$ and $ON=\sqrt{R^2-\left(CD/2\right)^2}$. When $AB>{}CD$, we have $\left(AB/2\right)^2>\left(CD/2\right)^2$, so $OM<{}ON$. $\blacksquare$

## Problem 13

(Exercise 29.2, Extension and Exploration, Problem 13) As shown in the figure, the railway $MN$ and the highway $PQ$ intersect at the point $O$, with $\angle{Q}ON=30^\circ$. There is a residential building at the point $A$, and $AO=200\,\text{m}$. If, when the train runs, the area within $200\,\text{m}$ around it is affected by noise, will the residential building be affected by noise as the train travels along $MN$ in the direction of $QN$? If the train's speed is $72\,\text{km/h}$, for how many seconds is the residential building affected by noise?

### Solution to Problem 13

By **Corollary 29.2.2 (Corollary to the Inscribed Angle Theorem)**.

Through $A$ draw $AB\perp{M}N$ at $B$. In $\text{Rt}\triangle{A}OB$, $\angle{A}OB=30^\circ$ and $AO=200$, so $AB=AO\sin30^\circ=100<200$, and the residential building will be affected by noise.

Draw a circle centered at $A$ with radius $200$, meeting $MN$ at $C$ and $D$. $BC=\sqrt{200^2-100^2}=100\sqrt3$, and $CD=200\sqrt3$. The train speed is $72\,\text{km/h}=20\,\text{m/s}$, so the duration of the effect is

$$
\frac{200\sqrt3}{20}=10\sqrt3\approx17.3\,\text{s}.
$$

$\blacksquare$

## Problem 14

(Exercise 29.2, Extension and Exploration, Problem 14) As shown in the figure, a harbor has a shoal within the range of $\overset{\frown}{AB}$. To keep deep-draft vessels out of the shoal, one measures the viewing angle $\angle{A}PB$ from the ship's position to the two lighthouses and compares it with the known danger angle $\angle{A}CB$, keeping $\angle{A}PB<\angle{A}CB$ while sailing. ### Solution to Problem 14

By **Theorem 29.2.3 (Inscribed Angle Theorem)**.

Draw a circle with chord $AB$; $\angle{A}CB$ is the inscribed angle on the shoal boundary. ## Exercise 10

(Exercise 29.3, Problem 1) Are two arcs of equal length equal arcs? Explain why.

### Solution to Exercise 10

By Arc Length Formula.

Not necessarily. Equal arcs require that they be able to coincide in the same circle or equal circles; when two arcs have equal length but lie in circles of different radii, the central angles they subtend differ, so they cannot coincide. $\blacksquare$

## Exercise 12

(Exercise 29.3, Problem 3) As shown in the figure, $\triangle{A}BC$ is an equilateral triangle of side length $a$; $D$, $E$, $F$ are the midpoints of $BC$, $CA$, $AB$ respectively. With the points $A$, $B$, $C$ as centers and radius $\frac{a}{2}$, draw circles. Find the area of the shaded part in the figure.

### Solution to Exercise 12

By Sector Area Formula.

All three sectors have central angle $60^\circ$ and radius $\frac{a}{2}$. The sum of the areas of the three sectors is

$$
3\cdot\frac{60\pi\left(a/2\right)^2}{360}=\frac{\pi{a}^2}{8}.
$$

The area of the equilateral triangle is $\frac{\sqrt3}{4}a^2$, so the shaded area is

$$
\frac{\sqrt3}{4}a^2-\frac{\pi{a}^2}{8}=\frac{a^2}{8}\left(2\sqrt3-\pi\right).
$$

$\blacksquare$

### Solution to Exercise 13

By Lateral and Total Surface Area of a Cone.

The base circumference is $2\pi\cdot6=12\pi$, which is the arc length of the sector in the lateral development. The sector radius is the generatrix $18$; by the arc length formula, $12\pi=\frac{n\pi\cdot18}{180}$, giving $n=120^\circ$. $\blacksquare$

## Problem 17

## Problem 18

(Exercise 29.3, Review and Consolidation, Problem 4) In $\text{Rt}\triangle{A}BC$, $\angle{C}=90^\circ$, $AC=3$, and $BC=4$. Rotate $\triangle{A}BC$ a full turn about each of its three sides. Find the total surface areas of the three resulting solids.

### Solution to Problem 18

By Lateral and Total Surface Area of a Cone.

$AB=5$.

Rotating about $AC$: a cone with base radius $4$ and generatrix $5$, total surface area $\pi\cdot4\cdot5+\pi\cdot4^2=36\pi$.

## Problem 22

(Exercise 29.3, Extension and Exploration, Problem 8) As shown in the figure, a sector with central angle $90^\circ$ is cut from a circular sheet of iron of diameter $1\,\text{m}$. Find the area of the remaining part. If the cut-out sector is formed into a cone, what is the radius of the cone's base circle?

### Solution to Problem 22

By Basic Knowledge and Basic Knowledge.

The circle radius is $0.5\,\text{m}$. The sector radius is $\frac{\sqrt2}{2}\,\text{m}$ (half the diagonal of the square). The sector area is $\frac{90\pi}{360}\cdot\left(\frac{\sqrt2}{2}\right)^2=\frac{\pi}{8}$. The circle area is $\pi\cdot0.5^2=\frac{\pi}{4}$, so the remaining area is $\frac{\pi}{4}-\frac{\pi}{8}=\frac{\pi}{8}\,\text{m}^2$.

The sector arc length is $l=\frac{90\pi}{180}\cdot\frac{\sqrt2}{2}=\frac{\sqrt2\pi}{4}$, so the cone's base radius is $r=\frac{l}{2\pi}=\frac{\sqrt2}{8}\approx0.18\,\text{m}$. $\blacksquare$
