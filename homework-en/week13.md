## Problem 2

(Problem 6.1, Comprehensive Application, Problem 9) As shown, a rectangular box has length, width, and height $a$, $b$, and $c$ respectively. An ant crawls along the surface from vertex $A$ to the opposite vertex $B$; find the length of the shortest path.

### Solution to Problem 2

By Net, unfold the sides of the box into a plane figure and then find the shortest path.

Unfold two adjacent faces of the box into the same plane; then the shortest path is the hypotenuse of a right triangle. There are three ways to unfold:

(1) Unfold the front and top: the legs are $\left(a+b\right)$ and $c$, and the path is $\sqrt{\left(a+b\right)^{2}+c^{2}}$.

(2) Unfold the front and right side: the legs are $a$ and $\left(b+c\right)$, and the path is $\sqrt{a^{2}+\left(b+c\right)^{2}}$.

(3) Unfold the left side and top: the legs are $\left(a+c\right)$ and $b$, and the path is $\sqrt{\left(a+c\right)^{2}+b^{2}}$.

Taking the smallest of the three gives the shortest path. $\blacksquare$

## Problem 3

(Problem 6.1, Extension and Exploration, Problem 10) Can you fold a square piece of paper into a triangular pyramid? Try it by hand.

### Solution to Problem 3

By Net and the symmetry of folding.

Fold the four corners of the square paper toward the center so that the four corner vertices meet at the center of the square. After folding, the four triangular faces form the lateral faces of a square pyramid with the center as the apex and the four sides of the square as the bases. If the square is folded twice along its two diagonals, then cut along the creases and unfolded, one obtains a net made of four triangular faces forming a triangular pyramid.

$\blacksquare$

## Problem 4

(Problem 6.1, Extension and Exploration, Problem 11) Cutting a cube with a plane, how many sides can the cross section have? Draw the shape of the cross section.

### Solution to Problem 4

By Plane Figure, the cross section is the plane figure obtained by the intersection of the plane with the cube's surface.

The plane intersects the cube's faces; each face it intersects gives one edge. The cube has six faces, so the cross section intersects at most six faces and is at most a hexagon. The cross section can be a triangle (equilateral, isosceles, or scalene), a quadrilateral (square, rectangle, or trapezoid), a pentagon, or a hexagon.

The figure shows the various possible cross sections obtained by cutting a cube with a plane: a triangle (equilateral, isosceles, or scalene), a quadrilateral (square, rectangle, or trapezoid), a pentagon, and a hexagon.

## Exercise 5

(Exercise 6.2, Problem 2) Point $C$ lies on segment $AB$, and points $M$ and $N$ are the midpoints of segments $AC$ and $BC$ respectively. If $MN=6$, find the length of segment $AB$.

### Solution to Exercise 5

By Midpoint of a Segment, $M$ is the midpoint of $AC$, so $MC=\frac{1}{2}AC$; $N$ is the midpoint of $BC$, so $CN=\frac{1}{2}BC$.

$$MN=MC+CN=\frac{1}{2}AC+\frac{1}{2}BC=\frac{1}{2}\left(AC+BC\right)=\frac{1}{2}AB.$$

Since $MN=6$, we have $AB=2\times6=12$. $\blacksquare$

## Problem 14

(Problem 6.2, Extension and Exploration, Problem 9) As shown, extend segment $AB$ to $C$ so that $BC=\frac{1}{2}AB$, then extend $BA$ to $D$ so that $AD=\frac{1}{2}AB$. If $DC=20\ \text{cm}$, find the length of $AB$.

### Solution to Problem 14

By Segment.

Let $AB=x$; then $BC=\frac{1}{2}x$ and $AD=\frac{1}{2}x$.

$$DC=AD+AB+BC=\frac{1}{2}x+x+\frac{1}{2}x=2x.$$

Since $DC=20$, we have $2x=20$, $x=10$. That is, $AB=10\ \text{cm}$. $\blacksquare$

## Problem 15

(Problem 6.2, Extension and Exploration, Problem 10) As shown, point $C$ lies on the extension of segment $AB$, and points $M$ and $N$ are the midpoints of segments $AC$ and $BC$ respectively. If $MN=5\ \text{cm}$, find the length of segment $AB$.

### Solution to Problem 15

By Midpoint of a Segment.

$M$ is the midpoint of $AC$, so $MC=\frac{1}{2}AC$. $N$ is the midpoint of $BC$, so $NC=\frac{1}{2}BC$. Since $C$ lies on the extension of $AB$,

$$MN=MC-NC=\frac{1}{2}AC-\frac{1}{2}BC=\frac{1}{2}\left(AC-BC\right)=\frac{1}{2}AB.$$

Since $MN=5$, we have $AB=2\times5=10\ \text{cm}$. $\blacksquare$

## Exercise 10

(Exercise 6.3, Problem 4) At $2$ o'clock and $15$ minutes on a clock, what is the angle between the hour hand and the minute hand?

### Solution to Exercise 10

By Measuring Angles, the hour hand turns $30^{\circ}$ per hour ($360^{\circ}\div12$) and $0.5^{\circ}$ per minute; the minute hand turns $6^{\circ}$ per minute ($360^{\circ}\div60$).

At $2$ o'clock and $15$ minutes, the minute hand is at: $15\times6^{\circ}=90^{\circ}$. The hour hand is at: $2\times30^{\circ}+15\times0.5^{\circ}=60^{\circ}+7.5^{\circ}=67.5^{\circ}$.

The angle: $90^{\circ}-67.5^{\circ}=22.5^{\circ}$. $\blacksquare$

## Exercise 14

(Exercise 6.3, Problem 1) As shown, the right-angle vertices of two set squares coincide at point $O$; find the degree measure of $\angle AOD+\angle BOC$.

### Solution to Exercise 14

By Comparing and Operating on Angles and Complementary Angle.

The right-angle vertices of the two set squares coincide, i.e. $\angle AOB=\angle COD=90^{\circ}$.

$$\angle AOD=\angle AOB+\angle BOD=90^{\circ}+\angle BOD.$$

$$\angle BOC=\angle COD-\angle BOD=90^{\circ}-\angle BOD.$$

$$\angle AOD+\angle BOC=\left(90^{\circ}+\angle BOD\right)+\left(90^{\circ}-\angle BOD\right)=180^{\circ}.$$

$\blacksquare$

## Exercise 15

(Exercise 6.3, Problem 2) The complement of an angle is $20^{\circ}$ less than $\frac{1}{3}$ of its supplement; find the degree measure of the angle.

### Solution to Exercise 15

By Complementary Angle and Supplementary Angle, let the angle be $x^{\circ}$.

Its complement is $\left(90-x\right)^{\circ}$, and its supplement is $\left(180-x\right)^{\circ}$.

$$90-x=\frac{1}{3}\left(180-x\right)-20.$$

Simplifying: $90-x=60-\frac{x}{3}-20$. $90-x=40-\frac{x}{3}$. $50=\frac{2x}{3}$. $x=75$. Hence the angle is $75^{\circ}$. $\blacksquare$

### Solution to Exercise 17

## Problem 16

(Problem 6.3, Review and Consolidation, Problem 1) In the figure below, which angles can be denoted by a single letter?

### Solution to Problem 16

By Angle, when there is only one angle at a vertex, it can be denoted by the single letter at that vertex.
