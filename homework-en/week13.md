> **Definition 6.1.1 (Solid Figure)**
> 
> A geometric figure whose parts do not all lie in the same plane is called a solid figure.

> **Definition 6.1.2 (Plane Figure)**
> 
> A geometric figure whose parts all lie in the same plane is called a plane figure.

> **Definition 6.1.3 (Net)**
> 
> Some solid figures are enclosed by several plane figures; if their surfaces are cut open appropriately, they can be unfolded into plane figures. Such a plane figure is called the net of the corresponding solid figure.

> **Definition 6.1.4 (Points, Lines, Planes, and Solids)**
> 
> A geometric solid is also called a solid for short. What encloses a solid is a surface; where two surfaces intersect forms a line; where two lines intersect is a point.

> **Definition 6.1.5 (A Moving Point Forms a Line, a Moving Line Forms a Plane, a Moving Plane Forms a Solid)**
> 
> A moving point forms a line, a moving line forms a plane, and a moving plane forms a solid.

> **Definition 6.2.1 (Line)**
> 
> Through two points there is one line, and only one line. In short: two points determine a line.

> **Definition 6.2.2 (Intersection)**
> 
> When two distinct lines have a common point, we say the two lines intersect, and this common point is called their intersection point.

> **Definition 6.2.3 (Ray and Segment)**
> 
> A point on a line and the part on one side of it is called a ray, and this point is called the endpoint of the ray. Two points on a line and the part between them is called a segment, and these two points are called the endpoints of the segment.

> **Definition 6.2.4 (Comparing Segments)**
> 
> To compare the lengths of two segments, one may measure their lengths with a ruler and compare, or move one segment onto the other for comparison.

> **Definition 6.2.5 (Basic Fact about Segments)**
> 
> Between two points, the segment is the shortest.

> **Definition 6.2.6 (Distance)**
> 
> The length of the segment joining two points is called the distance between them.

> **Definition 6.2.7 (Midpoint of a Segment)**
> 
> Point $M$ divides segment $AB$ into two equal segments $AM$ and $MB$; point $M$ is called the midpoint of segment $AB$.

> **Definition 6.3.1 (Angle)**
> 
> The figure formed by two rays with a common endpoint is called an angle.

> **Definition 6.3.2 (Measuring Angles)**
> 
> $1^{\circ}=60'$, $1'=60''$. A full angle $=360^{\circ}$.

> **Definition 6.3.3 (Comparing and Operating on Angles)**
> 
> To compare angles, one may measure their degrees with a protractor, or overlay the two angles. Addition and subtraction of angles are carried out based on their degrees.

> **Definition 6.3.4 (Angle Bisector)**
> 
> A ray starting from the vertex of an angle that divides the angle into two equal angles is called the angle bisector.

> **Definition 6.3.5 (Complementary Angle)**
> 
> If the sum of two angles equals $90^{\circ}$ (a right angle), we say the two angles are complementary to each other; that is, each angle is the complement of the other.

> **Definition 6.3.6 (Supplementary Angle)**
> 
> If the sum of two angles equals $180^{\circ}$ (a straight angle), we say the two angles are supplementary to each other; that is, each angle is the supplement of the other.

> **Definition 6.3.7 (Properties of Complementary and Supplementary Angles)**
> 
> Complements of the same angle (or of equal angles) are equal; supplements of the same angle (or of equal angles) are equal.

## Problem 9

(Problem 6.1, Comprehensive Application, Problem 9) As shown, a rectangular box has length, width, and height $a$, $b$, and $c$ respectively. An ant crawls along the surface from vertex $A$ to the opposite vertex $B$; find the length of the shortest path.

### Solution to Problem 9

By **Definition 6.1.3 (Net)**, unfold the sides of the box into a plane figure and then find the shortest path.

Unfold two adjacent faces of the box into the same plane; then the shortest path is the hypotenuse of a right triangle. There are three ways to unfold:

(1) Unfold the front and top: the legs are $\left(a+b\right)$ and $c$, and the path is $\sqrt{\left(a+b\right)^{2}+c^{2}}$.

(2) Unfold the front and right side: the legs are $a$ and $\left(b+c\right)$, and the path is $\sqrt{a^{2}+\left(b+c\right)^{2}}$.

(3) Unfold the left side and top: the legs are $\left(a+c\right)$ and $b$, and the path is $\sqrt{\left(a+c\right)^{2}+b^{2}}$.

Taking the smallest of the three gives the shortest path. $\blacksquare$

(Picture-Telling Math History: The Origins of Geometry) How did geometric knowledge arise from human production and daily life?

### Solution to Picture-Telling Math History 1

By **Definition 6.1.4 (Points, Lines, Planes, and Solids)**, geometry originates from human abstraction of the shapes, sizes, and positional relations of objects in the real world.

## Exercise 6

(Exercise 6.2, Problem 2) Point $C$ lies on segment $AB$, and points $M$ and $N$ are the midpoints of segments $AC$ and $BC$ respectively. If $MN=6$, find the length of segment $AB$.

### Solution to Exercise 6

By **Definition 6.2.7 (Midpoint of a Segment)**, $M$ is the midpoint of $AC$, so $MC=\frac{1}{2}AC$; $N$ is the midpoint of $BC$, so $CN=\frac{1}{2}BC$.

$$MN=MC+CN=\frac{1}{2}AC+\frac{1}{2}BC=\frac{1}{2}\left(AC+BC\right)=\frac{1}{2}AB.$$

Since $MN=6$, we have $AB=2\times6=12$. $\blacksquare$

(Problem 6.2, Extension and Exploration, Problem 7) As shown, point $C$ lies on segment $AB$, $AC=8\ \text{cm}$, $CB=6\ \text{cm}$, and points $M$ and $N$ are the midpoints of $AC$ and $BC$ respectively.

## Problem 21

(Problem 6.2, Extension and Exploration, Problem 9) As shown, extend segment $AB$ to $C$ so that $BC=\frac{1}{2}AB$, then extend $BA$ to $D$ so that $AD=\frac{1}{2}AB$. If $DC=20\ \text{cm}$, find the length of $AB$.

### Solution to Problem 21

By **Definition 6.2.3 (Ray and Segment)**.

Let $AB=x$; then $BC=\frac{1}{2}x$ and $AD=\frac{1}{2}x$.

$$DC=AD+AB+BC=\frac{1}{2}x+x+\frac{1}{2}x=2x.$$

Since $DC=20$, we have $2x=20$, $x=10$. That is, $AB=10\ \text{cm}$. $\blacksquare$

## Problem 22

(Problem 6.2, Extension and Exploration, Problem 10) As shown, point $C$ lies on the extension of segment $AB$, and points $M$ and $N$ are the midpoints of segments $AC$ and $BC$ respectively. If $MN=5\ \text{cm}$, find the length of segment $AB$.

### Solution to Problem 22

By **Definition 6.2.7 (Midpoint of a Segment)**.

$M$ is the midpoint of $AC$, so $MC=\frac{1}{2}AC$. $N$ is the midpoint of $BC$, so $NC=\frac{1}{2}BC$. Since $C$ lies on the extension of $AB$,

$$MN=MC-NC=\frac{1}{2}AC-\frac{1}{2}BC=\frac{1}{2}\left(AC-BC\right)=\frac{1}{2}AB.$$

Since $MN=5$, we have $AB=2\times5=10\ \text{cm}$. $\blacksquare$

(Reading and Thinking: Measuring Length) Why must units of length be unified?

### Solution to Reading and Thinking 1

By **Definition 6.2.6 (Distance)**, measuring length requires unified units.

Without unified units of length, different regions and different groups would use their own measurement standards, causing confusion in communication and trade. Unified units of length make measurement results comparable and consistent, and are the foundation of scientific research, production, and daily life. $\blacksquare$

## Exercise 8

(Exercise 6.3, Problem 1) As shown, point $O$ lies on line $AB$, $\angle AOC=50^{\circ}$; find the degree measure of $\angle BOC$.

### Solution to Exercise 8

By **Definition 6.3.6 (Supplementary Angle)**, $O$ lies on line $AB$, so $\angle AOB=180^{\circ}$, and $\angle AOC$ and $\angle BOC$ are supplementary.

$$\angle BOC=180^{\circ}-\angle AOC=180^{\circ}-50^{\circ}=130^{\circ}.$$

$\blacksquare$

## Exercise 9

(Exercise 6.3, Problem 2) Calculate:

(1) $48^{\circ}39'+67^{\circ}31'$;

(2) $90^{\circ}-28^{\circ}12'$;

(3) $22^{\circ}16'\times5$;

(4) $180^{\circ}\div8$.

### Solution to Exercise 9

By **Definition 6.3.2 (Measuring Angles)**, calculate using the base-$60$ system of degrees, minutes, and seconds.

(1) $48^{\circ}39'+67^{\circ}31'=115^{\circ}70'=116^{\circ}10'$.

(2) $90^{\circ}-28^{\circ}12'=89^{\circ}60'-28^{\circ}12'=61^{\circ}48'$.

(3) $22^{\circ}16'\times5=110^{\circ}80'=111^{\circ}20'$.

(4) $180^{\circ}\div8=22.5^{\circ}=22^{\circ}30'$. $\blacksquare$

## Exercise 10

(Exercise 6.3, Problem 3) Express $33.28^{\circ}$ in degrees, minutes, and seconds; express $108^{\circ}20'42''$ in degrees.

### Solution to Exercise 10

By **Definition 6.3.2 (Measuring Angles)**, convert using the base-$60$ system.

$$0.28^{\circ}=0.28\times60'=16.8',\quad 0.8'=0.8\times60''=48''.$$

Hence $33.28^{\circ}=33^{\circ}16'48''$.

$$42''=\frac{42}{60}'=0.7',\quad 20.7'=\frac{20.7}{60}^{\circ}=0.345^{\circ}.$$

Hence $108^{\circ}20'42''=108.345^{\circ}$. $\blacksquare$

## Exercise 11

(Exercise 6.3, Problem 4) At $2$ o'clock and $15$ minutes on a clock, what is the angle between the hour hand and the minute hand?

### Solution to Exercise 11

By **Definition 6.3.2 (Measuring Angles)**, the hour hand turns $30^{\circ}$ per hour ($360^{\circ}\div12$) and $0.5^{\circ}$ per minute; the minute hand turns $6^{\circ}$ per minute ($360^{\circ}\div60$).

At $2$ o'clock and $15$ minutes, the minute hand is at: $15\times6^{\circ}=90^{\circ}$. The hour hand is at: $2\times30^{\circ}+15\times0.5^{\circ}=60^{\circ}+7.5^{\circ}=67.5^{\circ}$.

The angle: $90^{\circ}-67.5^{\circ}=22.5^{\circ}$. $\blacksquare$
