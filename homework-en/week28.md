## Activity 1

(Mathematics Activity, Activity 1, Problem 1) Estimate the average height of the whole class. Steps:

(1) Survey and record the height of every student in the class, write each on an identical small slip of paper, and compute the class average height;

(2) Put all the slips into a box; after thorough stirring, randomly draw $15$ slips, compute the average of the heights on these $15$ slips, and then put them back;

(3) Compare the sample average height with the class average height;

(4) Repeat step (2) several times, comparing the sample average height each time with the class average height.

### Solution to Activity 1

Let the heights of the $N$ students in the class be $x_1,x_2,\ldots,x_N$, and the population average height be $\overline{x}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^N x_i$.

Each time a sample of size $15$ is drawn, the sample average is $\overline{x}_k=\frac{1}{15}\sum_{j=1}^{15}x_{i_j}$. By Simple Random Sampling, each draw is unbiased, and $\overline{x}_k$ fluctuates up and down around $\overline{x}$.

Comparison shows: the sample average generally does not equal the population average, but as the number of repetitions grows, the average of the sample averages approaches the population average. The larger the sample size, the more stably the sample average approaches the population average. $\blacksquare$

## Activity 2

(Mathematics Activity, Activity 2, Problem 1) Who has the faster reaction? Measure reaction speed with a ruler: a partner holds the ruler upright with the scale $0$ at the bottom, placed between the thumb and the other fingers; after releasing, grab the ruler as quickly as possible, and record the scale $d$ (cm) where the hand grabs it. Repeat the experiment $10$ times.

(1) Among the $10$ trials, what are the maximum, minimum and average of $d$?

(2) What is the relationship between $d$ and reaction speed?

(3) Swap with your partner and repeat; compare who reacts faster.

### Solution to Activity 2

Let the $10$ measured values be $d_1,d_2,\ldots,d_{10}$. The maximum is $d_{\max}=\max\left\{d_i\right\}$, the minimum is $d_{\min}=\min\left\{d_i\right\}$, and the average is $\overline{d}=\frac{1}{10}\sum_{i=1}^{10}d_i$.

The ruler falls in free fall, and the falling distance is $d=\frac{1}{2}gt^2$, so the reaction time is $t=\sqrt{\frac{2d}{g}}$. The smaller $d$ is, the shorter the reaction time $t$, and the faster the reaction.

Comparing the two people's $\overline d$: the one with the smaller $\overline d$ reacts faster. $\blacksquare$

(Comprehensive and Practical Activity, Activity 1, Problem 1) Investigate the changing pattern of daylight duration in Beijing in 2024. It is known that on Minor Cold (January 6) sunrise is $7:36$, sunset is $17:04$, and daylight is $9\ \text{h}\ 28\ \text{min}$; at the spring equinox daylight is about $12\ \text{h}$; at the summer solstice about $15\ \text{h}$; at the winter solstice about $9\ \text{h}\ 28\ \text{min}$.

(1) Calculate the daylight duration on Minor Cold;

The figure is a line chart with the 24 solar terms in order on the horizontal axis and daylight duration (hours) on the vertical axis, showing the variation of Beijing's daylight duration on each solar term day in 2024.

### Solution to Inquiry 1

(1) By Daylight Duration Formula, daylight duration $=$ (sunset $-$ noon) $\times2=\left(17:04-12:00\right)\times2=5\ \text{h}\ 04\ \text{min}\times2=10\ \text{h}\ 08\ \text{min}$. The table directly gives $9\ \text{h}\ 28\ \text{min}$; since noon is not exactly Beijing time $12:00$, there is a longitude error.

(2) Taking the order of the solar terms as the horizontal axis and daylight duration as the vertical axis, draw the line chart as shown.

(3) From the line chart, the daylight duration in Beijing gradually lengthens from the winter solstice to the summer solstice, and gradually shortens from the summer solstice to the winter solstice. ## Inquiry 2

(Comprehensive and Practical Activity, Activity 2, Problem 1) Compare the daylight duration on the 24 solar terms in 2024 among three places: Beijing, Artux in Xinjiang, and Jieyang in Guangdong.

(1) Observe the change of daylight duration in the three places and explain it with a statistical chart;

(2) What factors affect the change of daylight duration?

(3) If a place's daylight lengthens day by day, how do the sunrise and sunset times change?

### Solution to Inquiry 2

(1) The daylight duration of all three places shows an annual cycle of "short at the winter solstice, long at the summer solstice", but with different amplitudes: Artux is at a similar latitude to Beijing, and its daylight curve is similar in shape but overall delayed due to the different longitude; Jieyang is at a low latitude, and the difference between summer-solstice and winter-solstice daylight is small.

(2) The main factors affecting daylight duration are latitude (which determines the difference between summer-solstice and winter-solstice daylight) and date (which determines the position of the sun's direct rays). On the same date, the higher the latitude, the longer the summer-solstice daylight and the shorter the winter-solstice daylight.

(3) By Daylight Duration Formula, daylight duration $=\left($ noon $-$ sunrise $\right)\times2$. If daylight lengthens day by day, then (noon $-$ sunrise) grows, i.e. sunrise advances day by day; at the same time (sunset $-$ noon) grows, i.e. sunset is delayed day by day. $\blacksquare$

## Inquiry 3

(Comprehensive and Practical Activity, Activity 2, Problem 2) Given the daylight duration of a place, how can one infer the sunrise and sunset times? If the next day's sunrise time is known, how does one calculate the nighttime duration between the two days?

### Solution to Inquiry 3

By Daylight Duration Formula, given the daylight duration $T$ and taking noon as $12:00$, sunrise $=12:00-\frac{T}{2}$, and sunset $=12:00+\frac{T}{2}$.

The nighttime duration $=24\ \text{h}-T$. If the next day's sunrise time $t_{\text{出}}'$ is known, then the next day's nighttime duration $=t_{\text{出}}'$ (from the previous day's sunset to the next day's sunrise), i.e. $t_{\text{出}}'-$ the previous day's sunset time. $\blacksquare$

The phone obtains the user's latitude and longitude through positioning, and then uses an astronomical formula to calculate that day's sunrise and sunset times based on the date (the position of the sun's direct rays), thereby automatically turning the eye-care mode on or off. This is precisely the application of Daylight Duration Formula and the laws of the earth's revolution in daily life. $\blacksquare$

## Exercise 2

(Exercise 13.1, Problem 2) As shown, in $\triangle ABC$, $\angle BAC$ is a right angle, $AD\perp BC$ with foot at $D$, and point $E$ lies on segment $BD$. Find the acute triangles, right triangles and obtuse triangles in the figure.

## Exercise 3

(Exercise 13.2, Problem 1) Can the following three segments form a triangle? Why?

(1) $3$, $4$, $8$;

(2) $5$, $6$, $11$;

(3) $5$, $6$, $10$.

### Solution to Exercise 3

By Relationship Among the Three Sides of a Triangle, three segments form a triangle if and only if the sum of any two is greater than the third.

(1) $3+4=7< 8$; the sum of two sides is not greater than the third, so they cannot form a triangle.

(2) $5+6=11$, which is not greater than $11$, so they cannot form a triangle.

(3) $5+6=11> 10$, $5+10=15> 6$, $6+10=16> 5$; the sum of any two sides is greater than the third, so they can form a triangle. $\blacksquare$
