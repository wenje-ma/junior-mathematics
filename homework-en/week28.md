> **Definition 12.1.1 (Comprehensive Survey)**
> 
> A survey conducted on the entire population of objects.

> **Definition 12.1.2 (Population and Individual)**
> 
> The entire set of objects to be investigated is called the population; each object under investigation that makes up the population is called an individual.

> **Definition 12.1.3 (Sample Survey)**
> 
> Surveying only a part of the objects and then inferring the situation of the entire population from the survey data.

> **Definition 12.1.4 (Sample)**
> 
> A part of the individuals drawn from the population.

> **Definition 12.1.6 (Simple Random Sampling)**
> 
> Every individual in the population has an equal chance of being selected.

> **Definition 12.2.1 (Pie Chart)**
> 
> A circle represents the population, each sector represents a part of the population, and the size of the sector reflects that part's percentage of the population; the sector's central angle $=360^\circ\times$ percentage.

> **Definition 12.2.2 (Bar Chart)**
> 
> The height of a small rectangle represents the quantity of each category, making it easy to compare the sizes of the data.

> **Definition 12.2.3 (Line Chart)**
> 
> The rise or fall of a broken line represents the change of data over time.

> **Definition 12.2.4 (Frequency and Relative Frequency)**
> 
> The number of data falling in each group is called the frequency; the ratio of the frequency to the total number of data is called the relative frequency.

> **Definition 12.2.5 (Class Width and Number of Classes)**
> 
> The distance between the two endpoints of each group is called the class width; the number of groups is called the number of classes. Dividing the range by the class width, taking the integer and adding $1$ gives the number of classes.

> **Definition 12.2.6 (Frequency Distribution Histogram)**
> 
> The horizontal axis represents the data groups and the vertical axis represents the frequency, using the area of a small rectangle (the height when equally spaced) to reflect the frequency of each group.

> **Definition 12.2.7 (Trend Chart)**
> 
> A statistical chart using a line to describe the relationship between one quantity and another.

> **Formula 12.5.1 (Daylight Duration)**
> 
> Daylight duration $=$ (noon time $-$ sunrise time) $\times2=$ (sunset time $-$ noon time) $\times2$.

> **Definition 13.1.1 (Triangle)**
> 
> The figure formed by three segments not on the same line, joined end to end in order, is called a triangle.

> **Definition 13.1.2 (Sides, Vertices and Interior Angles)**
> 
> The segments making up a triangle are called the sides of the triangle; the common endpoint of two adjacent sides is called a vertex of the triangle; the angle formed by two adjacent sides is called an interior angle of the triangle, briefly an angle of the triangle. A triangle with vertices $A,B,C$ is denoted $\triangle ABC$.

> **Definition 13.1.3 (Isosceles Triangle)**
> 
> A triangle with two equal sides is called an isosceles triangle; the two equal sides are called the legs, the other side is called the base, the angle between the two legs is called the vertex angle, and the angle between a leg and the base is called a base angle.

> **Definition 13.1.4 (Equilateral Triangle)**
> 
> A triangle with all three sides equal is called an equilateral triangle; an equilateral triangle is a special isosceles triangle.

> **Definition 13.1.5 (Classification of Triangles by Sides)**
> 
> By the equality relation of sides, triangles are first divided into scalene triangles and isosceles triangles; isosceles triangles are further divided into isosceles triangles whose base and legs are unequal and equilateral triangles.

## Activity 1

(Mathematics Activity, Activity 1, Problem 1) Estimate the average height of the whole class. Steps:

(1) Survey and record the height of every student in the class, write each on an identical small slip of paper, and compute the class average height;

(2) Put all the slips into a box; after thorough stirring, randomly draw $15$ slips, compute the average of the heights on these $15$ slips, and then put them back;

(3) Compare the sample average height with the class average height;

(4) Repeat step (2) several times, comparing the sample average height each time with the class average height.

### Solution to Activity 1

Let the heights of the $N$ students in the class be $x_1,x_2,\ldots,x_N$, and the population average height be $\overline{x}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^N x_i$.

Each time a sample of size $15$ is drawn, the sample average is $\overline{x}_k=\frac{1}{15}\sum_{j=1}^{15}x_{i_j}$. By **Definition 12.1.6 (Simple Random Sampling)**, each draw is unbiased, and $\overline{x}_k$ fluctuates up and down around $\overline{x}$.

Comparison shows: the sample average generally does not equal the population average, but as the number of repetitions grows, the average of the sample averages approaches the population average. The larger the sample size, the more stably the sample average approaches the population average. $\blacksquare$

## Activity 2

(Mathematics Activity, Activity 2, Problem 1) Who has the faster reaction? Measure reaction speed with a ruler: a partner holds the ruler upright with the scale $0$ at the bottom, placed between the thumb and the other fingers; after releasing, grab the ruler as quickly as possible, and record the scale $d$ (cm) where the hand grabs it. Repeat the experiment $10$ times.

(1) Among the $10$ trials, what are the maximum, minimum and average of $d$?

(2) What is the relationship between $d$ and reaction speed?

(3) Swap with your partner and repeat; compare who reacts faster.

### Solution to Activity 2

Let the $10$ measured values be $d_1,d_2,\ldots,d_{10}$. The maximum is $d_{\max}=\max\left\{d_i\right\}$, the minimum is $d_{\min}=\min\left\{d_i\right\}$, and the average is $\overline{d}=\frac{1}{10}\sum_{i=1}^{10}d_i$.

The ruler falls in free fall, and the falling distance is $d=\frac{1}{2}gt^2$, so the reaction time is $t=\sqrt{\frac{2d}{g}}$. The smaller $d$ is, the shorter the reaction time $t$, and the faster the reaction.

Comparing the two people's $\overline d$: the one with the smaller $\overline d$ reacts faster. $\blacksquare$

## Inquiry 2

(Comprehensive and Practical Activity, Activity 2, Problem 1) Compare the daylight duration on the 24 solar terms in 2024 among three places: Beijing, Artux in Xinjiang, and Jieyang in Guangdong.

(1) Observe the change of daylight duration in the three places and explain it with a statistical chart;

(2) What factors affect the change of daylight duration?

(3) If a place's daylight lengthens day by day, how do the sunrise and sunset times change?

### Solution to Inquiry 2

(1) The daylight duration of all three places shows an annual cycle of "short at the winter solstice, long at the summer solstice", but with different amplitudes: Artux is at a similar latitude to Beijing, and its daylight curve is similar in shape but overall delayed due to the different longitude; Jieyang is at a low latitude, and the difference between summer-solstice and winter-solstice daylight is small.

(2) The main factors affecting daylight duration are latitude (which determines the difference between summer-solstice and winter-solstice daylight) and date (which determines the position of the sun's direct rays). On the same date, the higher the latitude, the longer the summer-solstice daylight and the shorter the winter-solstice daylight.

(3) By Daylight Duration Formula, daylight duration $=\left($ noon $-$ sunrise $\right)\times2$. If daylight lengthens day by day, then (noon $-$ sunrise) grows, i.e. sunrise advances day by day; at the same time (sunset $-$ noon) grows, i.e. sunset is delayed day by day. $\blacksquare$

## Inquiry 3

(Comprehensive and Practical Activity, Activity 2, Problem 2) Given the daylight duration of a place, how can one infer the sunrise and sunset times? If the next day's sunrise time is known, how does one calculate the nighttime duration between the two days?

### Solution to Inquiry 3

By Daylight Duration Formula, given the daylight duration $T$ and taking noon as $12:00$, sunrise $=12:00-\frac{T}{2}$, and sunset $=12:00+\frac{T}{2}$.

The nighttime duration $=24\ \text{h}-T$. If the next day's sunrise time $t_{\text{出}}'$ is known, then the next day's nighttime duration $=t_{\text{出}}'$ (from the previous day's sunset to the next day's sunrise), i.e. $t_{\text{出}}'-$ the previous day's sunset time. $\blacksquare$

## Exercise 2

(Exercise 13.1, Problem 2) As shown, in $\triangle ABC$, $\angle BAC$ is a right angle, $AD\perp BC$ with foot at $D$, and point $E$ lies on segment $BD$. Find the acute triangles, right triangles and obtuse triangles in the figure.

The figure shows a right triangle $ABC$ ($\angle BAC=90^\circ$), with $AD\perp BC$ at foot $D$, point $E$ on segment $BD$, and segment $AE$ drawn.

### Solution to Exercise 2

The triangles in the figure are $\triangle ABC$, $\triangle ABD$, $\triangle ADE$, $\triangle ADC$, $\triangle ABE$, $\triangle AEC$.

From $\angle BAC=90^\circ$, $\triangle ABC$ is a right triangle; from $AD\perp BC$, $\angle ADB=\angle ADE=\angle ADC=90^\circ$, so $\triangle ABD$, $\triangle ADE$, $\triangle ADC$ are all right triangles.

In $\triangle ADE$, $\angle ADE=90^\circ$, so the other two angles $\angle DAE$ and $\angle AED$ are both acute. The points $B,E,D,C$ lie in order on line $BC$; the direction from $E$ to $B$ is the same as the direction from $E$ to $D$, so $\angle AEB=\angle AED$ is acute, while $\angle AEC=180^\circ-\angle AED$ is obtuse.

In $\triangle ABE$, $\angle ABE=\angle ABC$ is acute ($\triangle ABC$ is a right triangle), and $\angle BAE$ and $\angle AEB$ are both acute, so $\triangle ABE$ is an acute triangle. In $\triangle AEC$, $\angle AEC$ is obtuse, so $\triangle AEC$ is an obtuse triangle.

In summary, the right triangles are $\triangle ABC$, $\triangle ABD$, $\triangle ADE$, $\triangle ADC$; the acute triangle is $\triangle ABE$; the obtuse triangle is $\triangle AEC$. $\blacksquare$

### Solution to Problem 10

In the right figure, points $B,D,E,C$ lie on the same line, point $A$ is outside this line, and $AB$, $AD$, $AE$, $AC$ are all drawn. Choosing any two points on line $BC$ together with point $A$ forms a triangle, giving $\binom{4}{2}=6$ in total.

They are $\triangle ABC$, $\triangle ABD$, $\triangle ABE$, $\triangle ADE$, $\triangle ADC$, $\triangle AEC$. Hence there are $6$ triangles in the figure. $\blacksquare$
