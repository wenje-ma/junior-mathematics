> **Definition 2.1.1 (Rules for Addition of Rational Numbers)**
> 
> When two numbers of the same sign are added, the sum takes the same sign; when two numbers of opposite signs are added, the sum takes the sign of the addend with the larger absolute value; adding two numbers that are opposites of each other gives $0$.

> **Definition 2.1.4 (Rules for Subtraction of Rational Numbers)**
> 
> Subtracting a number is equivalent to adding the opposite number of that number.

> **Definition 2.2.1 (Rules for Multiplication of Rational Numbers)**
> 
> When two numbers are multiplied, like signs give positive and opposite signs give negative, and the absolute value of the product equals the product of the absolute values of the factors.

> **Definition 2.2.2 (Reciprocal)**
> 
> Two numbers whose product is $1$ are reciprocals of each other.

> **Definition 2.2.3 (Commutative Law of Multiplication)**
> 
> When two numbers are multiplied, swapping the positions of the factors does not change the product, that is, $ab=ba$.

> **Definition 2.2.4 (Associative Law of Multiplication)**
> 
> When three numbers are multiplied, multiplying the first two first, or multiplying the last two first, does not change the product, that is, $\left(ab\right)c=a\left(bc\right)$.

> **Definition 2.2.5 (Distributive Law)**
> 
> Multiplying a number by the sum of two numbers is equivalent to multiplying that number separately by the two numbers and then adding the products, that is, $a\left(b+c\right)=ab+ac$.

> **Definition 2.2.6 (Sign Rules for Multiplying Several Rational Numbers)**
> 
> When several numbers not equal to $0$ are multiplied, the product is positive when the number of negative factors is even and negative when the number of negative factors is odd. When several numbers are multiplied and one of the factors is $0$, the product is $0$.

> **Definition 2.2.7 (Rules for Division of Rational Numbers)**
> 
> Dividing by a number not equal to $0$ is equivalent to multiplying by the reciprocal of that number.

> **Definition 2.3.1 (Powers)**
> 
> The operation of finding the product of $n$ identical factors is called exponentiation, and the result of exponentiation is called a power. In $a^{n}$, $a$ is called the base and $n$ is called the exponent.

> **Definition 2.3.2 (Sign Rules for Powers)**
> 
> An odd power of a negative number is negative, and an even power of a negative number is positive; any positive-integer power of a positive number is positive; any positive-integer power of $0$ is $0$.

> **Definition 2.3.3 (Order of Mixed Operations on Rational Numbers)**
> 
> First exponentiation, then multiplication and division, and finally addition and subtraction; operations at the same level are carried out from left to right; if there are parentheses, first carry out the operations inside the parentheses.

> **Definition 2.3.3 (Order of Mixed Operations)**
> 
> First exponentiation, then multiplication and division, and finally addition and subtraction; operations at the same level from left to right; if there are parentheses, first carry out the operations inside the parentheses.

> **Definition 2.3.4 (Scientific Notation)**
> 
> Expressing a number greater than $10$ in the form $a\times10^{n}$ ($1\le{a}<10$, where $n$ is a positive integer).

> **Definition 2.3.5 (Approximate Numbers)**
> 
> When taking an approximate number by the rounding method, one says that the approximate number is accurate to the digit to which it is rounded.

## Problem 1

(Exercise 2.2, Comprehensive Application, Problem 13) A helicopter starts from an altitude of $450\,\text{m}$, first rises vertically at a speed of $4\,\text{m/s}$ for $60\,\text{s}$, and then descends vertically at a speed of $5\,\text{m/s}$ for $120\,\text{s}$. What is the altitude of the helicopter at this point?

### Solution to Problem 1

By **Definition 2.2.1 (Rules for Multiplication of Rational Numbers)**, rising is positive and descending is negative.

$$450+4\times60-5\times120=450+240-600=90.$$

Hence the altitude of the helicopter at this point is $90\,\text{m}$. $\blacksquare$

## Problem 2

(Exercise 2.2, Extension and Exploration, Problem 14) Compute $2\times1$, $2\times\frac{1}{2}$, $2\times\left(-1\right)$, $2\times\left(-\frac{1}{2}\right)$. In connection with such concrete multiplications, do you think a nonzero rational number must be less than its $2$ times? Why?

### Solution to Problem 2

By **Definition 2.2.1 (Rules for Multiplication of Rational Numbers)**.

$$2\times1=2,\quad2\times\frac{1}{2}=1,\quad2\times\left(-1\right)=-2,\quad2\times\left(-\frac{1}{2}\right)=-1.$$

A nonzero rational number is not necessarily less than its $2$ times. When the number is positive, it is less than its $2$ times; when the number is negative, for instance $-1<2$ does not hold, because $2\times\left(-1\right)=-2<-1$, that is, the $2$ times of a negative number is smaller than the number itself. $\blacksquare$

## Problem 3

(Exercise 2.2, Extension and Exploration, Problem 15) Using the distributive law one obtains

$$-2\times6+3\times6=\left(-2+3\right)\times6,\quad-2\times\left(-5\right)+3\times\left(-5\right)=\left(-2+3\right)\times\left(-5\right).$$

If $a$ denotes any number, then using the distributive law, what is $-2a+3a$ equal to?

### Solution to Problem 3

By **Definition 2.2.5 (Distributive Law)**, use the distributive law in reverse.

$$-2a+3a=\left(-2+3\right)a=a.$$

Hence $-2a+3a=a$. $\blacksquare$

## Problem 4

(Exercise 2.2, Extension and Exploration, Problem 16) Compute $\left(-4\right)\div2$, $4\div\left(-2\right)$, $\left(-4\right)\div\left(-2\right)$. In connection with such concrete divisions, do you think the following formulas hold ($a$, $b$ are rational numbers and $b\ne0$)? What rule can be summed up from them?

$$\frac{-a}{b}=\frac{a}{-b}=-\frac{a}{b};\quad\frac{-a}{-b}=\frac{a}{b}.$$

### Solution to Problem 4

By **Definition 2.2.7 (Rules for Division of Rational Numbers)**.

$$\left(-4\right)\div2=-2,\quad4\div\left(-2\right)=-2,\quad\left(-4\right)\div\left(-2\right)=2.$$

From $\left(-4\right)\div2=4\div\left(-2\right)=-2$, it follows that $\frac{-a}{b}=\frac{a}{-b}=-\frac{a}{b}$ holds; from $\left(-4\right)\div\left(-2\right)=2=4\div2$, it follows that $\frac{-a}{-b}=\frac{a}{b}$ holds. The rule: among the three signs of a fraction — numerator, denominator, and fraction bar — changing any two of them leaves the value of the fraction unchanged. $\blacksquare$

## Inquiry and Discovery 1

(Inquiry and Discovery, Viewing the Rules for Multiplication of Rational Numbers from the Perspective of Extending the Number System) Reading material: From the perspective of extending the number system while preserving the consistency of the operational laws, the distributive law yields $1\times\left(-1\right)=-1$, $0\times\left(-1\right)=0$, $\left(-1\right)\times\left(-1\right)=1$.

### Solution to Inquiry and Discovery 1

By **Definition 2.2.5 (Distributive Law)**.

For $1\times\left(-1\right)$, if the distributive law holds, then $1\times\left(-1\right)+1\times1=1\times\left[\left(-1\right)+1\right]=1\times0=0$, and hence $1\times\left(-1\right)=-\left(1\times1\right)=-1$.

For $0\times\left(-1\right)$, similarly $0\times\left(-1\right)+0\times1=0\times0=0$, so $0\times\left(-1\right)=0$.

For $\left(-1\right)\times\left(-1\right)$, $\left(-1\right)\times\left(-1\right)+1\times\left(-1\right)=\left[\left(-1\right)+1\right]\times\left(-1\right)=0$, so $\left(-1\right)\times\left(-1\right)=-\left[1\times\left(-1\right)\right]=-\left(-1\right)=1$.

This shows that, in order to preserve the consistency of the operational laws, the rule "like signs give positive and opposite signs give negative" is reasonable. $\blacksquare$

## Problem 11

(Exercise 2.3, Comprehensive Application, Problem 8) A cuboid has length and width both equal to $a$ and height equal to $h$. How are its volume and surface area computed? When $a=2\,\text{cm}$ and $h=5\,\text{cm}$, what are its volume and surface area?

### Solution to Problem 11

The base area of the cuboid is $a^{2}$; the volume is the base area times the height, and the surface area is the two bases plus the four side faces.

$$V=a^{2}h,\quad{S}=2a^{2}+4ah.$$

When $a=2$ and $h=5$,

$$V=2^{2}\times5=20\,\text{cm}^{3};\quad{S}=2\times2^{2}+4\times2\times5=8+40=48\,\text{cm}^{2}.$$

Hence the volume is $20\,\text{cm}^{3}$ and the surface area is $48\,\text{cm}^{2}$. $\blacksquare$

## Problem 15

(Exercise 2.3, Extension and Exploration, Problem 12) Compute $\left(-2\right)^{2}$, $2^{2}$, $\left(-2\right)^{3}$, $2^{3}$. In connection with such concrete powers, do you think the following hold when $a<0$?

(1) $a^{2}>0$; (2) $a^{2}=\left(-a\right)^{2}$; (3) $a^{2}=-a^{2}$; (4) $a^{3}=-a^{3}$.

### Solution to Problem 15

By **Definition 2.3.2 (Sign Rules for Powers)**.

$$\left(-2\right)^{2}=4,\quad2^{2}=4,\quad\left(-2\right)^{3}=-8,\quad2^{3}=8.$$

(1) When $a<0$, $a^{2}$ is an even power of a negative number and is positive, so $a^{2}>0$ holds.

(2) $\left(-a\right)^{2}=\left(-2\right)^{2}=4=a^{2}$, so $a^{2}=\left(-a\right)^{2}$ holds.

(3) Since $a^{2}>0$ while $-a^{2}<0$, the two cannot be equal, so $a^{2}=-a^{2}$ does not hold.

(4) When $a<0$, $a^{3}<0$ and $-a^{3}>0$, the two cannot be equal, so $a^{3}=-a^{3}$ does not hold. $\blacksquare$

## Activity 1

(Math Activity, Activity 1, Organizing Household Income and Expenditure Accounts, Problem 1) Help a family record one month (or one week) of living income and expenditure accounts, recording income as positive and expenditure as negative; compute the total income, total expenditure, total balance, and average daily expenditure for the month (or week), and make reasonable suggestions for the family's expenditure.

### Solution to Activity 1

By **Definition 2.1.1 (Rules for Addition of Rational Numbers)**, the total income, total expenditure, and total balance are respectively the algebraic sums of the income and expenditure data.

Let the daily income/expenditure of a certain week be in turn $a_{1}$, $a_{2}$, $\cdots$, $a_{7}$ (income positive, expenditure negative). The total income is the sum of all the positive numbers, the total expenditure is the sum of the absolute values of all the negative numbers, and the total balance is the sum of the seven numbers. The average daily expenditure equals the total expenditure divided by the number of days, $7$. Comparing the proportions of the various expenditures, one can make reasonable suggestions for the family's expenditure. $\blacksquare$
