> **Definition 15.1.1 (Axisymmetric Figure)**
> 
> If a plane figure can be folded along a line so that the parts on both sides of the line coincide with each other, the figure is called an axisymmetric figure, and the line is called its axis of symmetry; the points that coincide after folding are called symmetric points. If folding one figure along a line makes it coincide with another figure, the two figures are said to be symmetric about that line.

> **Conclusion 15.1.1 (Properties of Axis Symmetry)**
> 
> Two figures symmetric about a line are congruent; the axis of symmetry is the perpendicular bisector of the segment joining any pair of symmetric points, i.e. the segment joining symmetric points is perpendicularly bisected by the axis of symmetry.

> **Definition 15.1.2 (Perpendicular Bisector of a Segment)**
> 
> The line passing through the midpoint of a segment and perpendicular to it is called the perpendicular bisector of the segment.

> **Conclusion 15.1.2 (Properties of the Perpendicular Bisector)**
> 
> A point on the perpendicular bisector of a segment is equidistant from the two endpoints of the segment.

> **Conclusion 15.1.3 (Determination of the Perpendicular Bisector)**
> 
> A point equidistant from the two endpoints of a segment lies on the perpendicular bisector of the segment. The perpendicular bisector of a segment can be regarded as the set of all points equidistant from the two endpoints.

> **Definition 15.1.3 (Converse Propositions)**
> 
> Two propositions whose hypothesis and conclusion are exactly reversed are called converse propositions; if the converse proposition of a theorem is proved to be true, then the two are converse theorems of each other.

> **Conclusion 15.2.1 (Drawing an Axisymmetric Figure)**
> 
> A geometric figure is composed of points; just draw the symmetric points about the axis of symmetry of some special points in the figure (such as segment endpoints), then join them in order, and a figure symmetric to the original figure about that line is obtained. To construct the symmetric point of a point about line $l$: through the point draw the perpendicular to $l$, cut off on the perpendicular a segment bisected by the foot, and the endpoint is the symmetric point.

> **Conclusion 15.2.2 (Axis Symmetry in Coordinates)**
> 
> The coordinates of the point symmetric to $\left(x,y\right)$ about the $x$-axis are $\left(x,-y\right)$; the coordinates of the point symmetric to $\left(x,y\right)$ about the $y$-axis are $\left(-x,y\right)$.

> **Definition 15.3.1 (Isosceles Triangle)**
> 
> A triangle with two equal sides is called an isosceles triangle; the two equal sides are called the legs, and the other side is called the base.

> **Conclusion 15.3.1 (Equal Sides Opposite Equal Angles)**
> 
> The two base angles of an isosceles triangle are equal.

> **Conclusion 15.3.2 (Three Lines in One)**
> 
> The median on the base, the altitude on the base, and the bisector of the vertex angle of an isosceles triangle coincide. An isosceles triangle is an axisymmetric figure, and the line of the median on the base is its axis of symmetry.

> **Conclusion 15.3.3 (Equal Angles Opposite Equal Sides)**
> 
> A triangle with two equal angles is an isosceles triangle.

> **Conclusion 15.3.4 (Equilateral Triangle)**
> 
> An equilateral triangle has three equal angles, each equal to $60^\circ$; a triangle with three equal angles is an equilateral triangle; an isosceles triangle with one angle equal to $60^\circ$ is an equilateral triangle.

> **Conclusion 15.3.5 (Right Triangle Containing a $30^\circ$ Angle)**
> 
> In a right triangle, if one acute angle equals $30^\circ$, then the leg opposite it equals half the hypotenuse.

## Exercise 5

(Exercise 15.1, Problem 3) Write the converse proposition of each of the following propositions, and determine whether these converse propositions hold.

(1) If two lines are parallel, corresponding angles are equal;

(2) if two real numbers are equal, then their absolute values are equal;

(3) corresponding angles of congruent triangles are equal.

### Solution to Exercise 5

(1) Converse proposition: if corresponding angles are equal, then two lines are parallel. Holds.

(2) Converse proposition: if the absolute values of two real numbers are equal, then the two real numbers are equal. Does not hold; for example, the absolute values of $5$ and $-5$ are equal but the numbers themselves are not.

(3) Converse proposition: two triangles with corresponding angles equal are congruent. Does not hold; triangles with three respectively equal angles have the same shape but not necessarily the same size. $\blacksquare$

## Exercise 15

(Exercise 15.3, Problem 3) Prove: if the median on one side of a triangle equals half of that side, then the triangle is a right triangle.

### Solution to Exercise 15

Suppose in $\triangle{A}BC$, $AD$ is the median on side $BC$, and $AD=\frac{1}{2}BC$. By the definition of median, $BD=DC=\frac{1}{2}BC$, so $AD=BD=DC$.

From $AD=BD$, $\triangle{A}BD$ is isosceles, $\angle{B}=\angle{B}AD$. From $AD=DC$, $\triangle{A}DC$ is isosceles, $\angle{C}=\angle{C}AD$.

Thus

$$
\angle{B}AC=\angle{B}AD+\angle{C}AD=\angle{B}+\angle{C}.
$$

Also $\angle{B}AC+\angle{B}+\angle{C}=180^\circ$, so $2\angle{B}AC=180^\circ$, i.e. $\angle{B}AC=90^\circ$. Therefore the triangle is a right triangle. $\blacksquare$

(Exercise 15.3, Problem 3) As shown, $AC$ and $BD$ intersect at point $O$, and $AB\parallel{C}D$, $OA=OB$. Prove that $OC=OD$.

### Solution to Exercise 18

From $OA=OB$, $\triangle{O}AB$ is isosceles; by Equal Sides Opposite Equal Angles, $\angle{A}=\angle{B}$.

From $AB\parallel{C}D$, the alternate interior angles are equal, giving $\angle{A}=\angle{C}$, $\angle{B}=\angle{D}$. Hence $\angle{C}=\angle{D}$.

By Equal Angles Opposite Equal Sides, $OC=OD$. $\blacksquare$

## Exercise 19

(Exercise 15.3, Problem 1) Draw the three axes of symmetry of an equilateral triangle. What can you discover?

### Solution to Exercise 19

An equilateral triangle is an axisymmetric figure; by Three Lines in One, the line of the median (altitude, angle bisector) on each side is an axis of symmetry. The three axes of symmetry meet at one point and divide the equilateral triangle into six congruent right triangles. $\blacksquare$

## Exercise 20

(Exercise 15.3, Problem 2) As shown, in equilateral triangle $ABC$, $AD$ is the altitude on $BC$, $\angle{B}DE=\angle{C}DF=60^\circ$. Which segments in the figure are equal to $BD$?

### Solution to Exercise 20

Since the triangle is equilateral and by Three Lines in One, $D$ is the midpoint of $BC$, $BD=DC$, and $AD\perp{B}C$. In $\text{Rt}\triangle{B}DE$, $\angle{B}=60^\circ$, $\angle{B}DE=60^\circ$, so all three angles of $\triangle{B}DE$ are $60^\circ$; it is equilateral, hence $BE=DE=BD$. Similarly, $\triangle{C}DF$ is also equilateral, $CF=DF=DC=BD$. Hence the segments equal to $BD$ are $DC$, $BE$, $DE$, $CF$, $DF$. $\blacksquare$
