> **Definition 8.1.1 (Square Root)**
> 
> If a number $x$ has square equal to $a$, i.e. $x^2=a$, then this number $x$ is called a square root (or second root) of $a$. The operation of finding the square root of a number is called extracting the square root, and extracting the square root and squaring are inverse operations of each other.

> **Definition 8.1.2 (Arithmetic Square Root)**
> 
> The positive square root $\sqrt{a}$ of a positive number $a$ is called the arithmetic square root of $a$, and $a$ is called the radicand. It is stipulated that the arithmetic square root of $0$ is $0$, i.e. $\sqrt0=0$. The square roots of a positive number $a$ are denoted by $\pm\sqrt{a}$.

> **Property 8.1.3 (Relationship Between Squaring and Extracting Roots)**
> 
> For any nonnegative number $a$, $\left(\sqrt{a}\right)^2=a$; for any number $a$, $\sqrt{a^2}=\left|a\right|$.

> **Definition 8.2.1 (Cube Root)**
> 
> If a number $x$ has cube equal to $a$, i.e. $x^3=a$, then this number $x$ is called a cube root (or third root) of $a$, denoted $\sqrt\left[3\right]{a}$, where $3$ is the index of the root. The operation of finding the cube root of a number is called extracting the cube root, and extracting the cube root and cubing are inverse operations of each other.

> **Property 8.2.2 (Cube Root of a Negative Number)**
> 
> $\sqrt\left[3\right]{-a}=-\sqrt\left[3\right]{a}$.

> **Definition 8.3.1 (Irrational Number)**
> 
> A non-terminating non-repeating decimal is also called an irrational number. Irrational numbers are divided into positive and negative.

> **Property 8.3.1 (Real Numbers and the Number Line)**
> 
> There is a one-to-one correspondence between real numbers and points on the number line. The real number represented by a point to the right on the number line is always larger than that represented by a point to the left.

> **Definition 8.3.2 (Real Number)**
> 
> Rational numbers and irrational numbers are collectively called real numbers. Real numbers are divided into positive real numbers, $0$, and negative real numbers.

> **Property 8.3.2 (Opposite Numbers and Absolute Value)**
> 
> The opposite number of a real number $a$ is $-a$. The absolute value of a positive real number is itself, the absolute value of a negative real number is its opposite number, and the absolute value of $0$ is $0$. The operation rules and properties of rational numbers also apply to real numbers.

## Problem 5

(Exercise 8.3, Review and Consolidation, Problem 5) Compute:

(1) $2\left(\sqrt6-\sqrt7\right)-\left|2\sqrt6-\sqrt7\right|$; (2) $\left(-\sqrt2\right)^2+\left(-\sqrt\left[3\right]{2}\right)^3$.

### Solution to Problem 5

(1) Is there a smallest positive integer? Is there a smallest integer?

(2) Is there a smallest rational number? Is there a smallest irrational number?

(3) Is there a smallest positive real number? Is there a smallest real number?

### Solution to Problem 6

By Real Numbers and the Number Line, real numbers can take continuous values on the number line.

## Problem 8

(Exercise 8.3, Comprehensive Application, Problem 8) As shown in the figure, the areas of two squares inside a rectangle are $3\;\text{cm}^2$ and $1\;\text{cm}^2$ respectively.

(1) Find the perimeter of the rectangle;

(2) Find the sum of the areas of the two shaded parts in the figure.

The figure shows a large rectangle containing two squares placed side by side: the larger square has area $3\;\text{cm}^2$ (side $\sqrt3$), the smaller square has area $1\;\text{cm}^2$ (side $1$); the two shaded regions are the parts of the large rectangle remaining after removing the two squares.

### Solution to Problem 8

The square of area $3\;\text{cm}^2$ has side length $\sqrt3\;\text{cm}$, and the square of area $1\;\text{cm}^2$ has side length $1\;\text{cm}$.

(1) The large rectangle has length $\sqrt3+1$ and width $\sqrt3$. Thus the perimeter is

$$
2\left[\left(\sqrt3+1\right)+\sqrt3\right]=2\left(2\sqrt3+1\right)=4\sqrt3+2\quad\left(\text{cm}\right).
$$

(2) The two shaded parts can be assembled into a small rectangle of length $\sqrt3-1$ and width $1$, so the sum of their areas is

$\blacksquare$

## Reading and Reflection 1

(Reading and Reflection: Why $\sqrt2$ Is Not a Rational Number) The ancient Greek Pythagorean school held that all quantities could be represented by integers or ratios of integers. Later it was discovered that the diagonal of a square of side length $1$ could not be represented by an integer or a ratio of integers. Explain why $\sqrt2$ is not a rational number.

### Solution to Reading and Reflection 1

Use proof by contradiction. Suppose $\sqrt2$ is rational; then there exist two coprime positive integers $p$, $q$ such that

$$
\frac{p}{q}=\sqrt2.
$$

Squaring both sides gives $p^2=2q^2$. Since $2q^2$ is even, $p^2$ is even, and only the square of an even number is even, so $p$ is also even. Let $p=2r$ ($r$ a positive integer); substituting gives $4r^2=2q^2$, i.e. $q^2=2r^2$, so $q$ is also even.

Thus both $p$ and $q$ are even, contradicting the assumption that $p$, $q$ are coprime. This contradiction shows that $\sqrt2$ cannot be written as a fraction, i.e. $\sqrt2$ is not a rational number. $\blacksquare$

## Activity 1

(Mathematical Activity, Activity 1) Estimate the length and width of $A0$ paper. According to the international standard, $A$-series paper is rectangular, and $A0$ paper has area $1\;\text{m}^2$. Fold $A0$ paper in half along its long side and cut it open to get $A1$ paper; fold $A1$ paper in half along its long side and cut it open to get $A2$ paper; continuing in this way gives $A3$, $A4$ paper. Based on this, can you estimate the length and width of $A0$ paper in millimeters respectively (take integers)?

### Solution to Activity 1

Let the length of $A0$ paper be $x$ and the width be $y$. After folding in half along the long side, the new paper ($A1$) has length $y$ and width $\frac{x}{2}$. All sizes of paper have the same shape (the length-to-width ratio is unchanged), so the ratio is equal before and after folding:

$$
\frac{x}{y}=\frac{y}{x/2}=\frac{2y}{x}.
$$

Thus $x^2=2y^2$, i.e. $x=\sqrt2\,y$. Also the area of $A0$ is $xy=1$; substituting gives

$$
\sqrt2\,y^2=1,\quad{y}^2=\frac{1}{\sqrt2},\quad{y}=\sqrt{\frac{1}{\sqrt2}}=2^{-\frac14}.
$$

From $2^{-\frac14}\approx0.8409\;\text{m}=841\;\text{mm}$, $x=\sqrt2\,y\approx1.1892\;\text{m}=1189\;\text{mm}$.

Thus $A0$ paper has length about $1189\;\text{mm}$ and width about $841\;\text{mm}$. $\blacksquare$

## Activity 2

(Mathematical Activity, Activity 2) Finding cube roots by mental arithmetic. The Chinese mathematician Hua Luogeng once blurted out that the cube root of $59319$ is $39$. Do you know how he calculated it so quickly?

(1) From $10^3=1000$, $100^3=1000000$, can you determine how many digits $\sqrt\left[3\right]{59319}$ has?

(2) Since the units digit of $59319$ is $9$, can you determine what the units digit of $\sqrt\left[3\right]{59319}$ is?

(3) If you delete the last three digits $319$ of $59319$ to get the number $59$, and $3^3=27$, $4^3=64$, can you determine what the tens digit of $\sqrt\left[3\right]{59319}$ is?

### Solution to Activity 2

By **Definition 8.2.1 (Cube Root)**, determine each digit using the pattern of cubes.

(1) $\because10^3=1000<59319<1000000=100^3$, $\therefore10<\sqrt\left[3\right]{59319}<100$, i.e. $\sqrt\left[3\right]{59319}$ is a two-digit number.

(2) The only units digit whose cube still has units digit $9$ is $9$ ($9^3=729$, units digit $9$), so the units digit of $\sqrt\left[3\right]{59319}$ is $9$.

(3) Deleting the last three digits $319$ gives $59$; $\because3^3=27<59<64=4^3$, $\therefore$ the cube root corresponding to the tens digit lies between $3$ and $4$; take the smaller $3$, i.e. the tens digit is $3$.

In summary, $\sqrt\left[3\right]{59319}=39$. $\blacksquare$

## Review Exercise 9

(Review Exercise, Comprehensive Application, Problem 9) On a clear day, the farthest distance $s$ (unit: $km$) a person standing by the sea can see over the ocean can be estimated by $s^2=16.74h$, where $h$ (unit: $m$) is the distance from the eye to sea level. Wang Fang stands by the sea observing, with her eye $1.5\,m$ above sea level; what is the farthest distance she can see in kilometers? If she stands on a rock by the sea, with her eye $8.7\,m$ above sea level, what is the farthest distance she can see in kilometers? (Keep two decimal places.)

### Solution to Review Exercise 9

From $s^2=16.74h$, $s=\sqrt{16.74h}$ (take the positive value).

When $h=1.5$, $s=\sqrt{16.74\times1.5}=\sqrt{25.11}\approx5.01\,km$.

When $h=8.7$, $s=\sqrt{16.74\times8.7}=\sqrt{145.64}\approx12.07\,km$.

Thus by the sea she can see about $5.01\,km$, and standing on the rock about $12.07\,km$. $\blacksquare$
