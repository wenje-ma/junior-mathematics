> **Definition 3.1.1 (Algebraic Expressions)**
> 
> An expression formed by connecting numbers or letters representing numbers with operation symbols is called an algebraic expression. A single number or letter alone is also an algebraic expression.

> **Definition 3.1.1 (Algebraic Expression)**
> 
> An expression formed by connecting numbers, or letters representing numbers, with operation symbols is called an algebraic expression.

> **Definition 3.1.2 (Forming Algebraic Expressions)**
> 
> Expressing the quantitative relationships in a problem as expressions containing numbers, letters, and operation symbols.

> **Definition 3.1.2 (Writing Algebraic Expressions)**
> 
> Expressing the quantitative relationships in a problem using expressions containing numbers, letters and operation symbols.

> **Definition 3.1.3 (Quantities in Inverse Proportion)**
> 
> Two related quantities: if one quantity changes and the other changes along with it, and the product of the two quantities is constant, then the two quantities are called quantities in inverse proportion, and the relationship between them is called an inverse proportion relationship. If the letters $x$ and $y$ are used to represent the two related quantities, and $k$ is used to represent their product ($k$ is a fixed value, and $k\ne0$), then the inverse proportion relationship can be expressed as $xy=k$.

> **Definition 3.2.1 (Value of an Algebraic Expression)**
> 
> The result obtained by substituting numerical values for the letters in an algebraic expression and computing according to the operations in the expression.

## Problem 2

(Exercise 3.1, Comprehensive Application, Problem 7) State what is different in the meaning of each of the following pairs of algebraic expressions, and give examples of the quantitative relationships in practical problems that they represent:

(1) $2m-1$ and $2\left(m-1\right)$;

(2) $\frac{1}{2}a$ and $\frac{1}{2}+a$.

### Solution to Problem 2

By **Definition 3.1.1 (Algebraic Expressions)**.

(1) $2m-1$ is $2$ times $m$ minus $1$; $2\left(m-1\right)$ is $2$ times the difference between $m$ and $1$. Example: if $m$ is a certain number, $2m-1$ is $1$ less than twice it; $2\left(m-1\right)$ first subtracts and then multiplies by $2$.

(2) $\frac{1}{2}a$ is half of $a$; $\frac{1}{2}+a$ is the sum of $a$ and $\frac{1}{2}$. Example: $\frac{1}{2}a$ represents half of $a$ yuan, and $\frac{1}{2}+a$ represents the quantity that is $\frac{1}{2}$ more than $a$. $\blacksquare$

## Problem 4

(Exercise 3.1, Comprehensive Application, Problem 10) (1) Let $n$ denote any integer. Use an algebraic expression containing $n$ to represent any even number and any odd number. (2) The units digit of a three-digit number is $a$, the tens digit is $b$, and the hundreds digit is $c$. Use an algebraic expression containing $a$, $b$, and $c$ to represent this three-digit number.

### Solution to Problem 4

By **Definition 3.1.2 (Forming Algebraic Expressions)**.

(1) Even numbers are all multiples of $2$, so any even number is $2n$; odd numbers are $1$ greater than the adjacent even number, so any odd number is $2n+1$.

(2) The hundreds digit $c$ represents $100c$, the tens digit $b$ represents $10b$, and the units digit $a$ represents $a$, so this three-digit number is $100c+10b+a$. $\blacksquare$

## Problem 5

(Exercise 3.1, Comprehensive Application, Problem 11) $3$ teams play a single round-robin tournament (each pair of teams plays one match). What is the total number of matches? What about $4$ teams? $5$ teams? $n$ teams?

### Solution to Problem 5

By **Definition 3.1.2 (Forming Algebraic Expressions)**.

For $3$ teams: $\frac{3\times2}{2}=3$ matches; for $4$ teams: $\frac{4\times3}{2}=6$ matches; for $5$ teams: $\frac{5\times4}{2}=10$ matches.

Each team plays one match against each of the remaining $\left(n-1\right)$ teams, giving $n\left(n-1\right)$ matches, but each match is counted once by each of the two teams, so for $n$ teams there are $\frac{n\left(n-1\right)}{2}$ matches in total. $\blacksquare$

## Problem 12

(Exercise 3.2, Comprehensive Application, Problem 7) $A$ and $B$ are $s\,\text{km}$ apart. Two people, Jia and Yi, drive from $A$ to $B$ at speeds of $a\,\text{km/h}$ and $b\,\text{km/h}$ respectively, and Jia takes less time.

By **Definition 3.1.2 (Forming Algebraic Expressions)**, time $=$ distance $\div$ speed.

(Math Activity, Activity 1, Puzzle-Building Game, Problem (1)) As shown in the figure, matchsticks are used to build a row of figures made up of triangles. If the figure contains $2$, $3$, or $4$ triangles, how many matchsticks are needed respectively? If the figure contains $n$ triangles, how many matchsticks are needed?

The figure shows a row of triangular figures built from matchsticks: triangles are placed side by side in a row, with adjacent triangles sharing a common side (the figure shows the cases with $2$, $3$, and $4$ triangles).

### Solution to Activity 1

By **Definition 3.1.2 (Forming Algebraic Expressions)**.

$1$ triangle requires $3$ matchsticks; each additional triangle shares one side with the previous triangle, so only $2$ more matchsticks are needed. Hence $2$ triangles require $3+2=5$ matchsticks, $3$ triangles require $5+2=7$ matchsticks, and $4$ triangles require $7+2=9$ matchsticks.

Following this pattern, $n$ triangles require $3+2\left(n-1\right)=2n+1$ matchsticks. $\blacksquare$

## Activity 2

(Math Activity, Activity 1, Puzzle-Building Game, Problem (2)) As shown in the figure, identical small squares are used to build a large square. Building the $1$-st square requires $4$ small squares, building the $2$-nd square requires $9$ small squares, and building the $3$-rd square requires $16$ small squares. Build and think: by this method, how many more small squares does the $n$-th square require than the $\left(n-1\right)$-th square?

The figure shows the $1$-st, $2$-nd, and $3$-rd squares built respectively from $2\times2$, $3\times3$, and $4\times4$ small squares.

### Solution to Activity 2

By **Definition 3.1.2 (Forming Algebraic Expressions)** and **Definition 3.2.1 (Value of an Algebraic Expression)**.

The $1$-st square is $2\times2=4$ small squares, the $2$-nd is $3\times3=9$, and the $3$-rd is $4\times4=16$. Hence the $n$-th square consists of $\left(n+1\right)^{2}$ small squares, and the $\left(n-1\right)$-th square consists of $n^{2}$ small squares.

The difference between the two is

$$\left(n+1\right)^{2}-n^{2}=n^{2}+2n+1-n^{2}=2n+1.$$

Hence the $n$-th square requires $2n+1$ more small squares than the $\left(n-1\right)$-th square. $\blacksquare$

## Review Problem 7

(Review Problem, Comprehensive Application, Problem 7) As shown in the figure, the side length of square $ABCD$ is $a$. Point $E$ lies on side $DC$, with $DE=b$, and the shaded region is triangle $ADE$.

(1) Based on the data in the figure, express the area $S$ of the shaded region using an algebraic expression containing $a$ and $b$;

(2) When $a=6$ and $b=2$, find the area of the shaded region.

### Solution to Review Problem 7

By **Definition 3.1.2 (Writing Algebraic Expressions)**.

(1) Triangle $ADE$ is a right triangle, with the two legs being $AD=a$ and $DE=b$, hence

$$S=\frac{1}{2}ab.$$

(2) By **Definition 3.2.1 (Value of an Algebraic Expression)**, when $a=6$ and $b=2$,

$$S=\frac{1}{2}\times6\times2=6.$$

Hence the area of the shaded region is $6$. $\blacksquare$
