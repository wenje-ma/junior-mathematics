> **Definition 3.1.1 (Algebraic Expression)**
> 
> An expression formed by connecting numbers, or letters representing numbers, with operation symbols is called an algebraic expression.

> **Definition 3.1.2 (Writing Algebraic Expressions)**
> 
> Expressing the quantitative relationships in a problem using expressions containing numbers, letters and operation symbols.

> **Definition 3.1.3 (Quantities in Inverse Proportion)**
> 
> Two related quantities: if one quantity changes and the other changes along with it, and the product of the two quantities is constant, then the two quantities are called quantities in inverse proportion, and the relationship between them is called an inverse proportion relationship. If the letters $x$ and $y$ are used to represent the two related quantities, and $k$ is used to represent their product ($k$ is a fixed value, and $k\ne0$), then the inverse proportion relationship can be expressed as $xy=k$.

> **Definition 3.2.1 (Value of an Algebraic Expression)**
> 
> The result obtained by substituting numerical values for the letters in an algebraic expression and computing according to the operations in the expression.

> **Definition 4.1.1 (Monomial)**
> 
> An algebraic expression formed by the product of numbers or letters is called a monomial. A single number or a single letter is also a monomial.

> **Definition 4.1.2 (Coefficient of a Monomial)**
> 
> The numerical factor in a monomial is called the coefficient of that monomial. When a monomial represents the product of a number and a letter, the number is usually written first; when the coefficient of a monomial is $1$ or $-1$, the $1$ is usually omitted.

> **Definition 4.1.3 (Degree of a Monomial)**
> 
> In a monomial, the sum of the exponents of all letters is called the degree of that monomial. If the degree of a monomial is $n$, it is called a monomial of degree $n$. For a nonzero number, its degree is defined to be $0$.

> **Definition 4.1.4 (Polynomial)**
> 
> The sum of several monomials is called a polynomial. Each monomial is called a term of the polynomial, and a term without a letter is called a constant term.

> **Definition 4.1.5 (Degree of a Polynomial)**
> 
> In a polynomial, the degree of the term with the highest degree is called the degree of the polynomial.

> **Definition 4.1.6 (Integral Expression)**
> 
> Monomials and polynomials are collectively called integral expressions.

> **Definition 4.2.1 (Like Terms)**
> 
> Terms that contain the same letters, with the same exponents on the same letters, are called like terms. Several constant terms are also like terms.

> **Definition 4.2.2 (Combining Like Terms)**
> 
> Combining like terms in a polynomial into a single term, the coefficient of the resulting term is the sum of the coefficients of the like terms before combining, while the letters together with their exponents remain unchanged.

> **Definition 4.2.3 (Rule for Removing Parentheses)**
> 
> When a number multiplies a polynomial, multiply the number outside the parentheses by each term inside them, then add the resulting products; in this way the parentheses can be removed. In particular, $+\left(x-3\right)=x-3$, $-\left(x-3\right)=-x+3$.

> **Definition 4.2.4 (Rule for Adding and Subtracting Integral Expressions)**
> 
> When adding or subtracting several integral expressions, first remove any parentheses, then combine like terms.

## Review Problem 1

(Review Problem, Comprehensive Application, Problem 8) As shown in the figure, a set of square-shaped figures is made with counters: the $1$-st uses $4$ counters, the $2$-nd uses $8$ counters, and the $3$-rd uses $12$ counters. Continuing in this way, how many counters are needed to make the $n$-th figure?

### Solution to Review Problem 1

By **Definition 3.1.2 (Writing Algebraic Expressions)**.

The $1$-st uses $4=4\times1$ counters, the $2$-nd $8=4\times2$ counters, the $3$-rd $12=4\times3$ counters; that is, each figure uses $4$ more counters than the previous one. Hence $4n$ counters are needed to make the $n$-th figure. $\blacksquare$

## Review Problem 2

(Review Problem, Comprehensive Application, Problem 9) Tanghulu. (1) If each bamboo skewer holds $5$ hawthorns, how many hawthorns are needed to make $n$ skewers of tanghulu? What kind of proportional relationship is there between the total number of hawthorns needed and the number of tanghulu skewers? (2) If $200$ hawthorns are used to make $b$ skewers of tanghulu, with the same number of hawthorns on each skewer, how many hawthorns are on each skewer? (3) If there are $a$ hawthorns and, under the rule that each skewer has the same number of hawthorns, $b$ skewers are made with $c$ hawthorns left over, how many hawthorns are on each skewer? When $a=130$, $b=16$, $c=2$, find the number of hawthorns on each skewer.

### Solution to Review Problem 2

By **Definition 3.1.2 (Writing Algebraic Expressions)** and **Definition 3.1.3 (Quantities in Inverse Proportion)**.

(1) Making $n$ skewers requires $5n$ hawthorns. The ratio of the total number of hawthorns to the number of skewers is $5$ (constant), so the two are in direct proportion.

(2) The number of hawthorns per skewer is $\frac{200}{b}$.

(3) A total of $\left(a-c\right)$ hawthorns were used, giving $\frac{a-c}{b}$ per skewer. When $a=130$, $b=16$, $c=2$, $\frac{a-c}{b}=\frac{130-2}{16}=8$, i.e. $8$ hawthorns per skewer. $\blacksquare$

If a team wins $a$ matches by $3\!:\!1$, wins $b$ matches by $3\!:\!2$, and loses $c$ matches by $2\!:\!3$, then this team's total score can be expressed by a polynomial as $\;\_\_\;$.

## Problem 9

(Problem 4.1, Extension and Exploration, Problem 9) Part of the shoe-size correspondence is as follows: new size $220$ corresponds to old size $34$, new size $225$ to old size $35$, new size $230$ to old size $36$, new size $235$ to old size $37$, ..., new size $270$ corresponds to old size $a$.

(1) Find the value of $a$;

(2) If the new size is $m$ and the old size is $n$, write a formula that converts the old size into the new size.

### Solution to Problem 9

By **Definition 4.1.4 (Polynomial)**, the correspondence between shoe sizes is a linear expression.

(1) Each increase of $5$ in the new size corresponds to an increase of $1$ in the old size. The new size goes from $235$ to $270$, an increase of $270-235=35$, i.e. $7$ groups of $5$, so the old size increases by $7$ groups of $1$ from $37$, giving $37+7=44$. Hence $a=44$.

(2) Let the old size be $n$ and the new size be $m$. From the correspondence data, each increase of $1$ in the old size corresponds to an increase of $5$ in the new size. When the old size is $34$, the new size is $220$, hence

$$m=220+5\left(n-34\right)=5n+50.$$

That is, the formula converting the old size to the new size is $m=5n+50$. $\blacksquare$

## Exercise 14

(Exercise 4.2, Problem 2) Find the value of $x^{2}-5xy-3x^{2}-2\left(1-2xy-x^{2}\right)$, where $x=-\frac{1}{9}$, $y=\frac{9}{2}$.

### Solution to Exercise 14

By **Definition 4.2.4 (Rule for Adding and Subtracting Integral Expressions)**, first simplify, then evaluate.

$$x^{2}-5xy-3x^{2}-2+4xy+2x^{2}=\left(1-3+2\right)x^{2}+\left(-5+4\right)xy-2=-xy-2.$$

## Exercise 15

(Exercise 4.2, Problem 3) The unit price of a notebook is $x$ yuan, and the unit price of a gel pen is $y$ yuan. Wang Fang bought $3$ notebooks and $2$ gel pens; Li Ming bought $4$ notebooks and $3$ gel pens. Buying these notebooks and gel pens, how much did Wang Fang and Li Ming spend in total?

### Solution to Exercise 15

By **Definition 4.2.4 (Rule for Adding and Subtracting Integral Expressions)**, the total cost is the sum of the two people's spending.

Wang Fang spent $\left(3x+2y\right)$ yuan, and Li Ming spent $\left(4x+3y\right)$ yuan. Hence the total cost is

$$\left(3x+2y\right)+\left(4x+3y\right)=7x+5y\,\text{元}.$$
