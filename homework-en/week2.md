> **Definition 1.1.1 (Positive Number)**
> 
> Numbers greater than $0$ are called positive numbers.

> **Definition 1.1.2 (Negative Number)**
> 
> Numbers obtained by prefixing a positive number with the sign "$-$" are called negative numbers.

> **Definition 1.1.5 (Representing Quantities of Opposite Meaning)**
> 
> Quantities of opposite meaning are represented by positive and negative numbers respectively.

> **Definition 1.2.1 (Integers and Fractions)**
> 
> Positive integers, $0$, and negative integers are collectively called integers; positive fractions and negative fractions are collectively called fractions.

> **Definition 1.2.2 (Rational Numbers)**
> 
> Numbers that can be written in fraction form are called rational numbers.

> **Definition 1.2.3 (Number Line)**
> 
> A straight line on which an origin, a positive direction, and a unit length are specified is called a number line.

> **Definition 1.2.4 (Opposite Numbers)**
> 
> Two numbers that differ only in sign are opposites of each other; the opposite number of $0$ is $0$.

> **Definition 1.2.5 (Absolute Value)**
> 
> The distance on the number line between the point representing a number $a$ and the origin is called the absolute value of the number $a$, denoted by $\left|a\right|$.

> **Definition 1.2.6 (Algebraic Meaning of Absolute Value)**
> 
> The absolute value of a positive number is itself; the absolute value of a negative number is its opposite number; the absolute value of $0$ is $0$.

> **Definition 1.2.7 (Comparing the Magnitude of Rational Numbers)**
> 
> A positive number is greater than $0$, $0$ is greater than a negative number, and a positive number is greater than a negative number; for two negative numbers, the one with the larger absolute value is instead the smaller.

## Exercise 1

(Exercise 1.2, Problem 2) Arrange each group of numbers below in order from smallest to largest, and connect them with "$<$":

(1) $-3$, $+2$, $+5$, $0$, $-10$, $8$;

(2) $-\frac{1}{4}$, $+2.3$, $-0.3$, $0$, $-\frac{3}{2}$, $-\frac{1}{2}$.

### Solution to Exercise 1

By **Definition 1.2.7 (Comparing the Magnitude of Rational Numbers)**, a negative number is less than $0$, and $0$ is less than a positive number; for two negative numbers, the one with the larger absolute value is instead the smaller.

(1) Among the negative numbers, $\left|-10\right|=10>\left|-3\right|=3$, so $-10<-3$; the positive numbers satisfy $0<2<5<8$. Hence arranged from smallest to largest:

$$-10<-3<0<+2<+5<8.$$

(2) The negative numbers are $-\frac{3}{2}=-1.5$, $-\frac{1}{2}=-0.5$, $-0.3$, $-\frac{1}{4}=-0.25$. Comparing absolute values: $1.5>0.5>0.3>0.25$, so $-1.5<-0.5<-0.3<-0.25$. Also $0<2.3$. Hence arranged from smallest to largest:

$$-\frac{3}{2}<-\frac{1}{2}<-0.3<-\frac{1}{4}<0<+2.3.$$

$\blacksquare$

## Exercise 2

(Exercise 1.2, Problem 3) Below are the average January temperatures of several cities in China in a certain year. Arrange these temperatures in order from highest to lowest.

Beijing $-4.6\,^\circ\text{C}$, Wuhan $3.8\,^\circ\text{C}$, Guangzhou $13.1\,^\circ\text{C}$, Harbin $-19.4\,^\circ\text{C}$, Nanjing $2.4\,^\circ\text{C}$.

### Solution to Exercise 2

By **Definition 1.2.7 (Comparing the Magnitude of Rational Numbers)**, a positive number is greater than $0$, $0$ is greater than a negative number; for two negative numbers, the one with the larger absolute value is instead the smaller.

The positive numbers are $3.8$, $13.1$, $2.4$, with $13.1>3.8>2.4$. The negative numbers are $-4.6$, $-19.4$; since $\left|-4.6\right|=4.6<\left|-19.4\right|=19.4$, we get $-4.6>-19.4$.

Hence arranged from highest to lowest:

$$13.1>3.8>2.4>-4.6>-19.4.$$

That is, Guangzhou $>$ Wuhan $>$ Nanjing $>$ Beijing $>$ Harbin. $\blacksquare$

## Problem 5

(Exercise 1.2, Review and Consolidation, Problem 5) Compare the magnitudes:

(1) $-\frac{2}{7}$ $\;\_\_\;$ $-\frac{4}{7}$;

(2) $-10$ $\;\_\_\;$ $-5$;

(3) $-\frac{2}{7}$ $\;\_\_\;$ $-\frac{4}{7}$;

(4) $-3$ $\;\_\_\;$ $-\frac{22}{7}$;

(5) $-\frac{5}{6}$ $\;\_\_\;$ $-\frac{6}{7}$;

(6) $-\left(-3\right)$ $\;\_\_\;$ $-\left|-3.01\right|$.

### Solution to Problem 5

By **Definition 1.2.7 (Comparing the Magnitude of Rational Numbers)**, for two negative numbers, the one with the larger absolute value is instead the smaller.

(1) $\left|-\frac{2}{7}\right|=\frac{2}{7}<\left|-\frac{4}{7}\right|=\frac{4}{7}$, so $-\frac{2}{7}>-\frac{4}{7}$.

(2) $\left|-10\right|=10>\left|-5\right|=5$, so $-10<-5$.

(3) $\left|-\frac{2}{7}\right|=\frac{2}{7}<\left|-\frac{4}{7}\right|=\frac{4}{7}$, so $-\frac{2}{7}>-\frac{4}{7}$.

(4) $\left|-3\right|=3=\frac{21}{7}$, $\left|-\frac{22}{7}\right|=\frac{22}{7}$; since $\frac{21}{7}<\frac{22}{7}$, we get $-3>-\frac{22}{7}$.

(5) $\left|-\frac{5}{6}\right|=\frac{5}{6}=\frac{35}{42}$, $\left|-\frac{6}{7}\right|=\frac{6}{7}=\frac{36}{42}$; since $\frac{35}{42}<\frac{36}{42}$, we get $-\frac{5}{6}>-\frac{6}{7}$.

(6) $-\left(-3\right)=3$, $-\left|-3.01\right|=-3.01$; a positive number is greater than a negative number, so $3>-3.01$, that is, $-\left(-3\right)>-\left|-3.01\right|$. $\blacksquare$

## Problem 7

(Exercise 1.2, Comprehensive Application, Problem 7) If one does not take care of one's eyes, nearsightedness may develop. During an eye examination, optometrists often record the degree of nearsightedness in the form "$\times\times\times\text{D}$"; for example, $50$ degrees of nearsightedness is recorded as "$-0.50\text{D}$", and $100$ degrees as "$-1.00\text{D}$", and so on. The eye-examination records of $6$ students are as follows:

$$-0.50\text{D},\,-1.25\text{D},\,-2.50\text{D},\,-0.75\text{D},\,-1.75\text{D},\,-2.25\text{D}.$$

Usually, when nearsightedness exceeds $200$ degrees, one must continuously wear glasses for vision correction. Among these $6$ students, how many need to continuously wear glasses?

### Solution to Problem 7

According to the problem, the records of nearsightedness degrees are negative numbers, and their absolute values are precisely the degrees of nearsightedness. By **Definition 1.2.6 (Algebraic Meaning of Absolute Value)**.

The absolute values of the records are respectively

$$0.50,\,1.25,\,2.50,\,0.75,\,1.75,\,2.25.$$

Nearsightedness exceeding $200$ degrees means the absolute value is greater than $2.00$. Among them, $2.50>2.00$ and $2.25>2.00$, totaling $2$.

Hence $2$ students need to continuously wear glasses. $\blacksquare$

## Problem 8

(Exercise 1.2, Extension and Exploration, Problem 8) On the number line, if points $A$ and $B$ represent two numbers that are opposites of each other, and the distance between these two points is $5$, then what are the numbers represented by these two points?

### Solution to Problem 8

By **Definition 1.2.4 (Opposite Numbers)**, two numbers $a$ and $-a$ that are opposites of each other are symmetric about the origin on the number line and are equidistant from the origin.

Let point $A$ represent $a$; then point $B$ represents $-a$. The distance between the two points is $\left|a-\left(-a\right)\right|=\left|2a\right|=5$.

Hence $\left|a\right|=\frac{5}{2}=2.5$, that is, $a=2.5$ or $a=-2.5$.

Hence the numbers represented by these two points are $2.5$ and $-2.5$. $\blacksquare$

## Problem 9

(Exercise 1.2, Extension and Exploration, Problem 9) If $a$ is a rational number, then under what condition on $a$ do the following hold:

(1) $a=-a$?

(2) $-a>{}a$?

(3) $-a<{}a$?

### Solution to Problem 9

By **Definition 1.2.4 (Opposite Numbers)** and **Definition 1.2.7 (Comparing the Magnitude of Rational Numbers)**.

(1) If $a=-a$, then $2a=0$, so $a=0$.

(2) If $-a>{}a$, rearranging gives $0>2a$, that is, $a<0$, so $a$ is a negative number.

(3) If $-a<{}a$, rearranging gives $0<2a$, that is, $a>0$, so $a$ is a positive number.

Hence (1) $a=0$; (2) $a$ is a negative number; (3) $a$ is a positive number. $\blacksquare$

## Activity 1

(Math Activity, Activity 1, Weight Survey, Problem 1) The standard weight (unit: $\text{kg}$) for a child or adolescent is computed as follows: standard weight $=$ (age $\times7-5\right)\div2$. The table below shows the weight situation of $6$ students in a group in Grade 7, where the number of kilograms exceeding the standard weight is recorded as a positive number and the number of kilograms short of the standard weight is recorded as a negative number.

Number $1$, $2$, $3$, $4$, $5$, $6$; weight situation $-1.1$, $+2$, $-0.5$, $+10$, $+4.7$, $-8.3$.

(1) Which students in the table have weights exceeding the standard weight? Analyze how the students in this group exceed or fall short of the standard weight.

(2) Which student in the table has the weight closest to the standard weight? To understand the weight situation of the students, besides judging positive and negative numbers, what else should one consider?

### Solution to Activity 1

By **Definition 1.1.5 (Representing Quantities of Opposite Meaning)**, exceeding the standard weight is recorded as a positive number, and falling short of the standard weight is recorded as a negative number.

(1) In the table, the positive numbers are $+2$, $+10$, $+4.7$, that is, students numbered $2$, $4$, $5$ have weights exceeding the standard weight; the negative numbers are $-1.1$, $-0.5$, $-8.3$, that is, students numbered $1$, $3$, $6$ have weights falling short of the standard weight. Among the $6$ students in the group, $3$ exceed and $3$ fall short; the total excess is $2+10+4.7=16.7\,\text{kg}$, and the total shortfall is $\left|-1.1\right|+\left|-0.5\right|+\left|-8.3\right|=9.9\,\text{kg}$; the group as a whole is somewhat overweight.

Besides judging positive and negative numbers, one should also consider the magnitude of the absolute value of the deviation, that is, the extent to which the weight deviates from the standard weight, in order to judge whether the weight is standard. $\blacksquare$

## Activity 2

(Math Activity, Activity 2, Number Guessing Game, Problem 1) Two people work together to complete the game according to the following steps:

(1) The first student silently thinks of an integer in the interval $-50\sim50$ and remembers it;

(2) The second student makes a guess about the number the first student is thinking of; the first student compares this number with the number in mind and then answers "too big", "too small", or "equal"; if equal, the second student has guessed correctly;

(3) If the second student has not guessed correctly, adjust the guess according to the first student's answer;

(4) Repeat steps (2) and (3) until the number is guessed.

Please play this game and think about how to guess so as to find the number faster. Play the game several more times to test whether your number-guessing strategy is effective.

### Solution to Activity 2

By **Definition 1.2.7 (Comparing the Magnitude of Rational Numbers)**, each answer "too big" or "too small" narrows the range in which the target number lies.

The number in mind lies in $-50\sim50$, giving $101$ integers in total. Using a bisection strategy, each time guess the midpoint of the current interval: first guess $0$; if "too big", the target lies in $-50\sim0$; if "too small", the target lies in $0\sim50$. Each time the interval length is reduced by about half; since $2^6=64<101\le128=2^7$, the number can be guessed in at most $7$ tries. Taking the midpoint each time rather than guessing arbitrarily guarantees that about half of the possible numbers are ruled out each time, and hence is the fastest. $\blacksquare$
