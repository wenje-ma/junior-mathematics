### Chapter 1 Rational Numbers

#### 1.1 Positive and Negative Numbers

**Definition 1.1.1** (Positive Number): A number greater than $0$ is called a positive number.

**Definition 1.1.2** (Negative Number): A number formed by adding the sign "$-$" before a positive number is called a negative number.

**Definition 1.1.5** (Representing Quantities of Opposite Meaning): Quantities of opposite meaning are represented respectively by positive and negative numbers.

#### 1.2 Rational Numbers and Their Comparison

**Definition 1.2.1** (Integers and Fractions): Positive integers, $0$, and negative integers are collectively called integers; positive fractions and negative fractions are collectively called fractions.

**Definition 1.2.2** (Rational Numbers): A number that can be written in fraction form is called a rational number. A number that can be written in positive fraction form is a positive rational number, and a number that can be written in negative fraction form is a negative rational number.

**Definition 1.2.3** (Number Line): A straight line that has an origin, a positive direction, and a unit length stipulated is called a number line. The origin divides the number line (excluding the origin) into the positive half-axis and the negative half-axis.

**Definition 1.2.4** (Opposite Numbers): Two numbers that differ only in sign are opposites of each other. The opposite number of $0$ is $0$. Adding a "$-$" sign before a number gives a new number that is the opposite number of the original number.

**Definition 1.2.5** (Absolute Value): The distance from the point representing the number $a$ on the number line to the origin is called the absolute value of the number $a$, denoted $\left|a\right|$.

**Definition 1.2.6** (Algebraic Meaning of Absolute Value): The absolute value of a positive number is itself; the absolute value of a negative number is its opposite number; the absolute value of $0$ is $0$.

**Definition 1.2.7** (Comparing the Magnitude of Rational Numbers): On the number line, the number on the left is less than the number on the right. Positive numbers are greater than $0$, $0$ is greater than negative numbers, and positive numbers are greater than negative numbers; for two negative numbers, the one with the larger absolute value is instead smaller.

### Chapter 2 Operations on Rational Numbers

#### 2.1 Addition and Subtraction of Rational Numbers

**Definition 2.1.1** (Rules for Addition of Rational Numbers): When two numbers of the same sign are added, the sum takes the same sign, and its absolute value equals the sum of the absolute values of the addends; when two numbers of opposite signs with unequal absolute values are added, the sum takes the sign of the addend with the larger absolute value, and its absolute value equals the difference between the larger and the smaller; adding two opposites gives $0$; adding $0$ to a number leaves the number unchanged.

**Definition 2.1.2** (Commutative Law of Addition): $a+b=b+a$.

**Definition 2.1.3** (Associative Law of Addition): $\left(a+b\right)+c=a+\left(b+c\right)$.

**Definition 2.1.4** (Rules for Subtraction of Rational Numbers): Subtracting a number is equivalent to adding the opposite number of that number, namely $a-b=a+\left(-b\right)$.

#### 2.2 Multiplication and Division of Rational Numbers

**Definition 2.2.1** (Rules for Multiplication of Rational Numbers): When two numbers are multiplied, like signs give positive and opposite signs give negative, and the absolute value of the product equals the product of the absolute values of the factors. Multiplying any number by $0$ gives $0$.

**Definition 2.2.2** (Reciprocal): Two numbers whose product is $1$ are reciprocals of each other.

**Definition 2.2.3** (Commutative Law of Multiplication): $ab=ba$.

**Definition 2.2.4** (Associative Law of Multiplication): $\left(ab\right)c=a\left(bc\right)$.

**Definition 2.2.5** (Distributive Law): $a\left(b+c\right)=ab+ac$.

**Definition 2.2.6** (Sign Rules for Multiplying Several Rational Numbers): When several numbers not equal to $0$ are multiplied, the product is positive when the number of negative factors is even and negative when it is odd; if any factor is $0$, the product is $0$.

**Definition 2.2.7** (Rules for Division of Rational Numbers): Dividing by a number not equal to $0$ is equivalent to multiplying by the reciprocal of that number, namely $a\div b=a\cdot\frac{1}{b}$ ($b\ne 0$). Like signs give positive and opposite signs give negative; dividing $0$ by any number not equal to $0$ gives $0$.

#### 2.3 Powers of Rational Numbers

**Definition 2.3.1** (Powers): The operation of finding the product of $n$ identical factors is called exponentiation, and the result is called a power. $a$ is called the base and $n$ the exponent. It is stipulated that when $a\ne 0$, $a^{0}=1$.

**Definition 2.3.2** (Sign Rules for Powers): An odd power of a negative number is negative, and an even power of a negative number is positive; any positive-integer power of a positive number is positive; any positive-integer power of $0$ is $0$.

**Definition 2.3.3** (Order of Mixed Operations): First exponentiation, then multiplication and division, and finally addition and subtraction; operations at the same level from left to right; if there are parentheses, first carry out the operations inside.

**Definition 2.3.4** (Scientific Notation): Expressing a number greater than $10$ in the form $a\times 10^{n}$ ($1\le a< 10$, where $n$ is a positive integer).

**Definition 2.3.5** (Approximate Numbers): Taking an approximate number by rounding, one says it is accurate to whichever digit it is rounded to.

### Chapter 3 Algebraic Expressions

#### 3.1 Forming Algebraic Expressions to Represent Quantitative Relationships

**Definition 3.1.1** (Algebraic Expressions): An expression formed by connecting numbers or letters representing numbers with operation symbols is called an algebraic expression. A single number or letter alone is also an algebraic expression.

**Definition 3.1.2** (Forming Algebraic Expressions): Expressing the quantitative relationships in a problem as expressions containing numbers, letters, and operation symbols.

**Definition 3.1.3** (Quantities in Inverse Proportion): Two related quantities: if one changes and the other changes along with it, and the product of the two is constant, namely $xy=k$ ($k$ is a fixed value, $k\ne 0$), then the two quantities are in an inverse proportion relationship.

#### 3.2 Value of an Algebraic Expression

**Definition 3.2.1** (Value of an Algebraic Expression): The result obtained by substituting numerical values for the letters in an algebraic expression and computing according to the operations in the expression. When the letters take different numerical values, the value of the algebraic expression generally also differs.

### Chapter 4 Addition and Subtraction of Integral Expressions

#### 4.1 Integral Expressions

**Definition 4.1.1** (Monomial): An algebraic expression formed by the product of numbers or letters is called a monomial. A single number or a single letter alone is also a monomial.

**Definition 4.1.2** (Coefficient of a Monomial): The numerical factor in a monomial is called the coefficient of that monomial. When a monomial represents the product of a number and letters, the number is usually written first; when the coefficient is $1$ or $-1$, the $1$ is usually omitted.

**Definition 4.1.3** (Degree of a Monomial): In a monomial, the sum of the exponents of all letters is called the degree of that monomial. The degree of a nonzero number is stipulated to be $0$.

**Definition 4.1.4** (Polynomial): The sum of several monomials is called a polynomial. Each monomial is called a term of the polynomial, and a term containing no letter is called a constant term. Each term includes the plus or minus sign in front of it.

**Definition 4.1.5** (Degree of a Polynomial): The degree of the term with the highest degree in a polynomial is called the degree of that polynomial.

**Definition 4.1.6** (Integral Expressions): Monomials and polynomials are collectively called integral expressions.

#### 4.2 Addition and Subtraction of Integral Expressions

**Definition 4.2.1** (Like Terms): Terms that contain the same letters and have the same exponents for the same letters are called like terms. Several constant terms are also like terms.

**Definition 4.2.2** (Combining Like Terms): Combining the like terms in a polynomial into one term; the coefficient of the resulting term is the sum of the coefficients of the like terms before combining, and the letters along with their exponents remain unchanged.

**Definition 4.2.3** (Rules for Removing Parentheses): Multiply each term inside the parentheses by the number outside, then add the resulting products, thereby removing the parentheses. $+\left(x-3\right)=x-3$, $-\left(x-3\right)=-x+3$.

**Definition 4.2.4** (Rules for Addition and Subtraction of Integral Expressions): When adding or subtracting several integral expressions, if there are parentheses, first remove them, and then combine like terms.

### Chapter 5 Linear Equations in One Unknown

#### 5.1 Equations

**Definition 5.1.1** (Equation): First set up a letter to represent the unknown, then according to the equality relationship in the problem, write an equality containing the unknown; such an equality is called an equation.

**Definition 5.1.2** (Solution of an Equation): The value of the unknown that makes the values on the left and right sides of the equation equal is called the solution of the equation. The process of finding the solution of an equation is called solving the equation.

**Definition 5.1.3** (Linear Equation in One Unknown): An equation that contains only one unknown (variable), the expressions containing the unknown are all integral expressions, and the degree of the unknown is $1$, is called a linear equation in one unknown.

**Definition 5.1.4** (Property of Equality 1): Adding (or subtracting) the same number (or expression) to both sides of an equality, the result remains equal. If $a=b$, then $a\pm c=b\pm c$.

**Definition 5.1.5** (Property of Equality 2): Multiplying both sides of an equality by the same number, or dividing by the same number not equal to $0$, the result remains equal. If $a=b$, then $ac=bc$; if $a=b$, $c\ne0$, then $\frac{a}{c}=\frac{b}{c}$.

#### 5.2 Solving Linear Equations in One Unknown

**Definition 5.2.1** (Solving Equations by Combining Like Terms): Combine the terms containing the unknown in the equation, combine the constant terms, and reduce the equation to the form $ax=b$ ($a\ne0$).

**Definition 5.2.2** (Transposition of Terms): Moving a term from one side of an equality to the other while changing its sign is called transposition. Transposition is based on Property of Equality 1.

**Definition 5.2.3** (General Steps for Solving a Linear Equation in One Unknown): Remove denominators, remove parentheses, transpose terms, combine like terms, and make the coefficient equal to $1$.

#### 5.3 Practical Problems and Linear Equations in One Unknown

**Definition 5.3.1** (Steps for Solving Word Problems by Setting Up Equations): Set up the unknown, set up the equation, solve the equation, check and give the answer.

**Common Models**: Matching problems (proportional relationships), work problems (superposition of efficiencies), sales profit/loss (profit $=$ selling price $-$ cost), sports scores (accumulated points), plan selection (cost comparison).

### Chapter 6 Introduction to Geometric Figures

#### 6.1 Geometric Figures

**Definition 6.1.1** (Solid Figures): A geometric figure whose parts do not all lie in the same plane is called a solid figure.

**Definition 6.1.2** (Plane Figures): A geometric figure whose parts all lie in the same plane is called a plane figure.

**Definition 6.1.3** (Nets): Appropriately cutting open the surface of a solid figure allows it to be unfolded into a plane figure, called the net of the corresponding solid figure.

**Definition 6.1.4** (Points, Lines, Surfaces, Solids): A geometric solid is also called a solid for short. What encloses a solid is a surface; where two surfaces intersect forms a line; where two lines intersect is a point.

**Definition 6.1.5** (A Point Moving Forms a Line, a Line Moving Forms a Surface, a Surface Moving Forms a Solid): A point moving forms a line, a line moving forms a surface, and a surface moving forms a solid.

#### 6.2 Straight Lines, Rays, Line Segments

**Definition 6.2.1** (Straight Line): Through two points there is one straight line, and only one straight line. Briefly: two points determine one straight line.

**Definition 6.2.2** (Intersection): When two different straight lines have a common point, the two lines are said to intersect, and this common point is called their intersection.

**Definition 6.2.3** (Ray, Line Segment): A point on a straight line and the part on one side of it is called a ray, and this point is called the endpoint of the ray. Two points on a straight line and the part between them is called a line segment, and these two points are called the endpoints of the line segment.

**Definition 6.2.4** (Comparison of Line Segments): To compare the lengths of two line segments, one may use a ruler to measure their lengths and compare, or move one line segment onto the other for comparison.

**Definition 6.2.5** (Basic Fact about Line Segments): Among all the paths connecting two points, the line segment is the shortest. Briefly: between two points, the line segment is the shortest.

**Definition 6.2.6** (Distance): The length of the line segment connecting two points is called the distance between the two points.

**Definition 6.2.7** (Midpoint of a Line Segment): The point $M$ divides the line segment $AB$ into two equal line segments $AM$ and $MB$, and the point $M$ is called the midpoint of the line segment $AB$.

#### 6.3 Angles

**Definition 6.3.1** (Angle): A figure formed by two rays with a common endpoint is called an angle. This common endpoint is the vertex of the angle, and the two rays are the two sides of the angle.

**Definition 6.3.2** (Measurement of Angles): Dividing a full angle into $360$ equal parts, each part is an angle of $1$ degree, denoted $1^{\circ}$. $1^{\circ}=60'$, $1'=60''$.

**Definition 6.3.3** (Comparison and Operations on Angles): To compare the size of angles, one may use a protractor to measure the degrees and compare, or superpose two angles. Addition and subtraction of angles are based on their degree measures.

**Definition 6.3.4** (Angle Bisector): A ray starting from the vertex of an angle and dividing the angle into two equal angles is called the bisector of that angle.

**Definition 6.3.5** (Complementary Angles): If the sum of two angles equals $90^{\circ}$ (a right angle), the two angles are said to be complementary.

**Definition 6.3.6** (Supplementary Angles): If the sum of two angles equals $180^{\circ}$ (a straight angle), the two angles are said to be supplementary.

**Definition 6.3.7** (Properties of Complementary and Supplementary Angles): Complements of the same angle (or equal angles) are equal; supplements of the same angle (or equal angles) are equal.

### Chapter 7 Intersecting and Parallel Lines

#### 7.1 Intersecting Lines

**Definition 7.1.1** (Adjacent Supplementary Angles)

Among the four angles formed by two intersecting straight lines, two angles that have one common side and whose other sides are opposite extensions of each other are adjacent supplementary angles. Adjacent supplementary angles are supplementary, that is, their sum is $180^\circ$.

**Definition 7.1.2** (Vertical Angles)

Among the four angles formed by two intersecting straight lines, two angles that have a common vertex and whose sides are respectively the opposite extensions of each other's sides are vertical angles.

**Theorem 7.1.1** (Vertical Angles Are Equal)

Vertical angles are equal.

**Definition 7.1.3** (Perpendicular)

When one of the four angles formed by two intersecting straight lines is a right angle, the two lines are said to be perpendicular to each other, denoted $a\perp b$. One of the lines is called the perpendicular of the other, and the intersection is called the foot of the perpendicular.

**Basic Fact 7.1.1** (Perpendicular Lines)

In the same plane, through a given point there is exactly one straight line perpendicular to a given straight line.

**Theorem 7.1.2** (The Perpendicular Segment Is the Shortest)

Among all the line segments connecting a point outside a line to points on the line, the perpendicular segment is the shortest. Briefly: the perpendicular segment is the shortest.

**Definition 7.1.4** (Distance from a Point to a Line)

The length of the perpendicular segment from a point outside a line to the line is called the distance from the point to the line.

**Definition 7.1.5** (Corresponding Angles, Alternate Interior Angles, Consecutive Interior Angles)

Two straight lines are cut by a third line: a pair of angles on the same side of the two lines and on the same side of the transversal is called corresponding angles; a pair of angles between the two lines and on opposite sides of the transversal is called alternate interior angles; a pair of angles between the two lines and on the same side of the transversal is called consecutive interior angles.

#### 7.2 Parallel Lines

**Definition 7.2.1** (Parallel)

In the same plane, two non-intersecting straight lines are called parallel lines, denoted $a\parallel b$. In the same plane, two non-coincident straight lines have only two positional relationships: intersecting and parallel.

**Basic Fact 7.2.1** (Parallel Axiom)

Through a point outside a straight line there is exactly one straight line parallel to that line.

**Theorem 7.2.1** (Transitivity of Parallelism)

If two straight lines are both parallel to a third line, then the two lines are also parallel to each other. That is, if $b\parallel a$, $c\parallel a$, then $b\parallel c$.

**Basic Fact 7.2.2** (Criterion 1)

Two straight lines are cut by a third line; if the corresponding angles are equal, then the two lines are parallel. Briefly: corresponding angles equal, the two lines are parallel.

**Theorem 7.2.2** (Criterion 2)

Two straight lines are cut by a third line; if the alternate interior angles are equal, then the two lines are parallel. Briefly: alternate interior angles equal, the two lines are parallel.

**Theorem 7.2.3** (Criterion 3)

Two straight lines are cut by a third line; if the consecutive interior angles are supplementary, then the two lines are parallel. Briefly: consecutive interior angles supplementary, the two lines are parallel.

**Property 7.2.1** (Property 1)

Two parallel straight lines are cut by a third line; the corresponding angles are equal. Briefly: two lines parallel, corresponding angles equal.

**Property 7.2.2** (Property 2)

Two parallel straight lines are cut by a third line; the alternate interior angles are equal. Briefly: two lines parallel, alternate interior angles equal.

**Property 7.2.3** (Property 3)

Two parallel straight lines are cut by a third line; the consecutive interior angles are supplementary. Briefly: two lines parallel, consecutive interior angles supplementary.

#### 7.3 Definitions, Propositions, Theorems

**Definition 7.3.1** (Definition)

A clear and explicit description of a mathematical object that reveals its essential characteristics is called a definition of the mathematical object.

**Definition 7.3.2** (Proposition)

A declarative sentence that can be judged to be true or false is called a proposition. One judged true is called a true proposition, and one judged false is called a false proposition. A proposition is often written in the form "if... then..."; "if" is followed by the hypothesis, and "then" by the conclusion.

**Definition 7.3.3** (Theorem)

A true proposition whose correctness has been verified through reasoning is called a theorem. A theorem can also serve as a basis for further reasoning.

**Definition 7.3.4** (Proof)

The correctness of a proposition needs reasoning to be judged; this reasoning process is called a proof.

**Method 7.3.1** (Giving a Counterexample)

To judge that a proposition is false, one only needs to give a counterexample: it satisfies the hypothesis of the proposition but does not satisfy the conclusion.

#### 7.4 Translation

**Definition 7.4.1** (Translation)

In a plane, moving a figure a certain distance in a certain direction is called translation of the figure. The direction of translation is not limited to horizontal or vertical; it may be along any direction in the plane.

**Property 7.4.1** (Properties of Translation)

(1) Before and after translation, the new figure and the original figure have exactly the same shape and size; (2) every point in the new figure is obtained by moving a certain point in the original figure (corresponding point), and the line segments connecting each pair of corresponding points are parallel (or on the same straight line) and equal.

### Chapter 8 Real Numbers

#### 8.1 Square Roots

**Definition 8.1.1** (Square Roots)

If the square of a number $x$ equals $a$, namely $x^2=a$, then this number $x$ is called a square root (or second root) of $a$. The operation of finding the square root of a number is called taking the square root; taking the square root and squaring are inverse operations.

**Property 8.1.1** (Properties of Square Roots)

A positive number has two square roots, which are opposites of each other; the square root of $0$ is $0$; a negative number has no square root.

**Definition 8.1.2** (Principal Square Root)

The positive square root $\sqrt a$ of a positive number $a$ is called the principal square root of $a$, and $a$ is called the radicand. It is stipulated that the principal square root of $0$ is $0$, namely $\sqrt 0=0$. The square roots of a positive number $a$ are denoted $\pm\sqrt a$.

**Property 8.1.2** (Magnitude of the Principal Square Root)

The larger the radicand, the larger the corresponding principal square root (this holds for all positive numbers).

**Property 8.1.3** (Relationship Between Squaring and Taking Square Roots)

For any nonnegative number $a$, $\left(\sqrt a\right)^2=a$; for any number $a$, $\sqrt{a^2}=\left|a\right|$.

#### 8.2 Cube Roots

**Definition 8.2.1** (Cube Roots)

If the cube of a number $x$ equals $a$, namely $x^3=a$, then this number $x$ is called a cube root (or third root) of $a$, denoted $\sqrt\left[3\right]{a}$, where $3$ is the root index. The operation of finding the cube root of a number is called taking the cube root; taking the cube root and cubing are inverse operations.

**Property 8.2.1** (Properties of Cube Roots)

The cube root of a positive number is positive, the cube root of a negative number is negative, and the cube root of $0$ is $0$.

**Property 8.2.2** (Cube Root of a Negative Number)

$\sqrt\left[3\right]{-a}=-\sqrt\left[3\right]{a}$.

**Property 8.2.3** (Relationship Between Cubing and Taking Cube Roots)

For any number $a$, $\left(\sqrt\left[3\right]{a}\right)^3=a$; $\sqrt\left[3\right]{a^3}=a$.

#### 8.3 Real Numbers and Their Simple Operations

**Definition 8.3.1** (Irrational Numbers)

Non-terminating non-repeating decimals are also called irrational numbers. Irrational numbers are divided into positive and negative.

**Definition 8.3.2** (Real Numbers)

Rational numbers and irrational numbers are collectively called real numbers. Real numbers are divided into positive real numbers, $0$, and negative real numbers.

**Property 8.3.1** (Real Numbers and the Number Line)

Real numbers are in one-to-one correspondence with points on the number line. The real number represented by a point on the right of the number line is always larger than that on the left.

**Property 8.3.2** (Opposite Numbers and Absolute Value)

The opposite number of a real number $a$ is $-a$. The absolute value of a positive real number is itself, the absolute value of a negative real number is its opposite number, and the absolute value of $0$ is $0$. The operation rules and properties of rational numbers also apply to real numbers.

### Chapter 9 Cartesian Coordinate System

#### 9.1 Describing the Position of Points in the Plane by Coordinates

**Definition 9.1.1** (Cartesian Coordinate System)

Drawing two mutually perpendicular number lines with a common origin in a plane forms a Cartesian coordinate system. The horizontal number line is called the $x$-axis, taking right as the positive direction; the vertical number line is called the $y$-axis, taking up as the positive direction; the intersection of the two axes is called the origin.

**Definition 9.1.2** (Coordinates of a Point)

From a point in the plane, draw perpendiculars respectively to the $x$-axis and $y$-axis; the coordinate of the foot on the $x$-axis is the abscissa of the point, and the coordinate on the $y$-axis is the ordinate of the point; the ordered pair $\left(x,y\right)$ is called the coordinates of this point.

**Property 9.1.1** (One-to-One Correspondence Between Points and Coordinates)

Points in the coordinate plane are in one-to-one correspondence with ordered pairs of real numbers.

**Definition 9.1.3** (Quadrants)

The coordinate plane is divided into four parts by the two coordinate axes; each part is called a quadrant, starting from the upper right and going in turn the first, second, third, and fourth quadrants. The first quadrant $\left(+,+\right)$, the second quadrant $\left(-,+\right)$, the third quadrant $\left(-,-\right)$, the fourth quadrant $\left(+,-\right)$. Points on the coordinate axes do not belong to any quadrant.

#### 9.2 Simple Applications of Coordinate Methods

**Method 9.2.1** (Representing Geographic Locations by Coordinates)

Establish a Cartesian coordinate system, choose an appropriate reference point as the origin, determine the positive directions and unit length of the $x$-axis and $y$-axis, and then the positions of various locations in the region can be represented by coordinates.

**Property 9.2.1** (Representing Translation by Coordinates)

In the Cartesian coordinate system, translating the point $\left(x,y\right)$ to the right (left) by $a$ unit length gives the corresponding point $\left(x+a,y\right)$ (respectively $\left(x-a,y\right)$); translating up (down) by $b$ unit length gives the corresponding point $\left(x,y+b\right)$ (respectively $\left(x,y-b\right)$). Adding (subtracting) $a$ to all the abscissas of a figure translates the figure to the right (left) by $a$ units; adding (subtracting) $a$ to all the ordinates translates the figure up (down) by $a$ units.

### Chapter 10 Systems of Linear Equations in Two Unknowns

#### 10.1 Concepts of Systems of Linear Equations in Two Unknowns

**Definition 10.1.1** (Linear Equation in Two Unknowns)

An equation that contains two unknowns, the expressions containing the unknown are all integral expressions, and the degree of each term containing the unknown is $1$, is called a linear equation in two unknowns.

**Definition 10.1.2** (System of Linear Equations in Two Unknowns)

A system of equations that contains two unknowns, the expressions containing the unknown are all integral expressions, the degree of each term containing the unknown is $1$, and there are altogether two equations, is called a system of linear equations in two unknowns.

**Definition 10.1.3** (Solution of a Linear Equation in Two Unknowns)

The values of the two unknowns that make the values on both sides of a linear equation in two unknowns equal are called the solution of that equation.

**Definition 10.1.4** (Solution of a System of Linear Equations in Two Unknowns)

The common solution of the two equations of a system of linear equations in two unknowns is called the solution of that system.

#### 10.2 Elimination — Solving Systems of Linear Equations in Two Unknowns

**Definition 10.2.1** (Elimination Idea)

The idea of reducing the number of unknowns in a system of linear equations from many to few and solving them one by one is called the elimination idea.

**Definition 10.2.2** (Substitution Elimination Method)

Express one unknown in one equation of a system in terms of an expression containing the other unknown, and then substitute it into the other equation to achieve elimination, thereby finding the solution of the system. This method is called the substitution elimination method, or substitution method for short.

**Definition 10.2.3** (Addition-Subtraction Elimination Method)

When the coefficients of the same unknown in two equations are opposite or equal, adding or subtracting the two sides of the two equations respectively eliminates that unknown and gives a linear equation in one unknown. This method is called the addition-subtraction elimination method, or addition-subtraction method for short.

#### 10.3 Practical Problems and Systems of Linear Equations in Two Unknowns

**Method 10.3.1** (Solving Word Problems by Setting Up a System of Equations)

Read the problem and set up the unknown, find two equality relationships, set up a system of linear equations in two unknowns, solve the system, check and give the answer.

#### 10.4 Solving Systems of Linear Equations in Three Unknowns

**Definition 10.4.1** (System of Linear Equations in Three Unknowns)

A system that contains three unknowns, the expressions containing the unknown are all integral expressions, the degree of each term containing the unknown is $1$, and there are altogether three equations, is called a system of linear equations in three unknowns.

**Method 10.4.2** (Solving a System of Linear Equations in Three Unknowns)

Through substitution elimination or addition-subtraction elimination, first eliminate one unknown, reduce the system of three unknowns to a system of two unknowns, then reduce to a linear equation in one unknown, and solve them one by one.

### Chapter 11 Inequalities and Systems of Inequalities

#### 11.1 Inequalities

**Definition 11.1.1** (Inequality)

An expression representing a magnitude relationship with the symbols "$< $" or "$> $" is called an inequality.

**Definition 11.1.2** (Solution of an Inequality)

The value of the unknown that makes an inequality hold is called a solution of the inequality.

**Definition 11.1.3** (Solution Set of an Inequality)

All the solutions of an inequality containing an unknown form the solution set of that inequality.

**Definition 11.1.4** (Solving an Inequality)

The process of finding the solution set of an inequality is called solving the inequality.

**Fact 11.1.1** (Basic Fact about Inequalities 1)

If $a> b$, then $b< a$.

**Fact 11.1.2** (Basic Fact about Inequalities 2)

If $a> b$, $b> c$, then $a> c$.

**Property 11.1.1** (Property of Inequalities 1)

Adding (or subtracting) the same number (or expression) to both sides of an inequality, the direction of the inequality sign remains unchanged. That is, if $a> b$, then $a\pm c> b\pm c$.

**Property 11.1.2** (Property of Inequalities 2)

Multiplying (or dividing) both sides of an inequality by the same positive number, the direction of the inequality sign remains unchanged. That is, if $a> b$, $c> 0$, then $ac> bc$.

**Property 11.1.3** (Property of Inequalities 3)

Multiplying (or dividing) both sides of an inequality by the same negative number, the direction of the inequality sign changes. That is, if $a> b$, $c< 0$, then $ac< bc$.

**Definition 11.1.5** ($\ge$ and $\le$)

"$\ge$" is read as "greater than or equal to" (i.e., not less than), and "$\le$" is read as "less than or equal to" (i.e., not greater than).

#### 11.2 Linear Inequalities in One Unknown

**Definition 11.2.1** (Linear Inequality in One Unknown)

An inequality that contains only one unknown, the expressions containing the unknown are all integral expressions, and the degree of the unknown is $1$, is called a linear inequality in one unknown.

**Method 11.2.2** (Solving a Linear Inequality in One Unknown)

Remove denominators, remove parentheses, transpose terms, combine like terms, and make the coefficient equal to $1$; note that when making the coefficient equal to $1$ by multiplying or dividing by a negative number, the direction of the inequality sign changes.

#### 11.3 Systems of Linear Inequalities in One Unknown

**Definition 11.3.1** (System of Linear Inequalities in One Unknown)

Combining several linear inequalities in one unknown forms a system of linear inequalities in one unknown.

**Definition 11.3.2** (Solution Set of a System of Inequalities)

The common part of the solution sets of several inequalities is called the solution set of the system they form.

**Method 11.3.3** (Solving a System of Inequalities)

First solve each inequality separately, then use the number line to determine the common part of the solution sets.

### Chapter 12 Data Collection, Organization, and Description

#### 12.1 Statistical Surveys

**Definition 12.1.1** (Comprehensive Survey)

A survey conducted on the entire population is called a comprehensive survey.

**Definition 12.1.2** (Population and Individual)

The entire set of objects to be investigated is called the population; each object making up the population is called an individual.

**Definition 12.1.3** (Sampling Survey)

Surveying only a part of the objects and then inferring the situation of the entire population from the survey data is called a sampling survey.

**Definition 12.1.4** (Sample)

A part of the individuals drawn from the population is called a sample of the population.

**Definition 12.1.5** (Sample Size)

The number of individuals in a sample is called the sample size.

**Definition 12.1.6** (Simple Random Sampling)

In the process of drawing a sample, every individual in the population has an equal chance of being drawn; such a sampling method is called simple random sampling.

#### 12.2 Describing Data with Statistical Charts

**Definition 12.2.1** (Bar Chart)

A statistical chart that uses the heights of bars to represent the magnitude of data in each category, intuitively showing the amount of data in each category and facilitating comparison of differences between categories.

**Definition 12.2.2** (Pie Chart)

A statistical chart that uses the size of sectors in a circle to represent the percentage of each part of the whole, showing the percentage and relative size of each category.

**Definition 12.2.3** (Line Chart)

A statistical chart that connects data points with line segments, often used to describe data changing over time, showing the trend of data over time.

**Definition 12.2.4** (Frequency)

The number of data falling into each group is called the frequency.

**Definition 12.2.5** (Class Width and Number of Classes)

The distance between the two endpoints of each group (the range of values within the group) is called the class width; the number of groups is called the number of classes. The difference between the maximum and minimum divided by the class width determines the number of classes.

**Definition 12.2.6** (Frequency Distribution Histogram)

A statistical chart that uses the horizontal axis for groups and the vertical axis for frequency, reflecting the frequency of each group by the area (or height, for equal-width grouping) of small rectangles.

**Definition 12.2.7** (Trend Chart)

A statistical chart that uses a line (straight or curved) to describe the relationship between one quantity and another, facilitating prediction of the trend of one quantity from the change of the other.

#### 12.5 Comprehensive Practice: Inquiry into the Pattern of Daylight Duration

**Formula 12.5.1** (Daylight Duration)

Daylight duration $=$ (noon time $-$ sunrise time) $\times2=$ (sunset time $-$ noon time) $\times2$. Noon is taken as local noon exactly at $12$ o'clock.

**Conclusion 12.5.2** (Pattern of Daylight Variation)

At the same location, daylight duration varies with an annual cycle: shortest at the winter solstice, longest at the summer solstice; when daylight lengthens day by day, sunrise advances and sunset delays; the higher the latitude, the greater the difference in daylight duration between the summer and winter solstices.

### Chapter 13 Triangles

#### 13.1 Concept of a Triangle

**Definition 13.1.1** (Triangle): A figure formed by three line segments not on the same straight line, connected end to end in sequence, is called a triangle; the line segments forming the triangle are called sides, the common endpoint of two adjacent sides is called a vertex, and the angle formed by two adjacent sides is called an interior angle of the triangle.

**Conclusion 13.1.1** (Classification of Triangles): By sides, triangles are classified into scalene triangles and isosceles triangles (an equilateral triangle is a special isosceles triangle); by angles, into acute triangles, right triangles, and obtuse triangles.

**Conclusion 13.1.2** (Relationship Among the Three Sides of a Triangle): The sum of two sides of a triangle is greater than the third side; the difference between two sides is less than the third side.

#### 13.2 Line Segments Associated with a Triangle

**Definition 13.2.1** (Altitude of a Triangle): Drawing a perpendicular from a vertex of a triangle to the line containing the side opposite it, the line segment between the vertex and the foot is called the altitude on that side.

**Definition 13.2.2** (Median of a Triangle): The line segment connecting a vertex of a triangle and the midpoint of the opposite side is called the median on that side. The three medians of a triangle meet at one point (the centroid).

**Definition 13.2.3** (Angle Bisector of a Triangle): The bisector of an interior angle of a triangle intersects the opposite side; the line segment between the vertex of that angle and the intersection is called the angle bisector of the triangle.

#### 13.3 Interior and Exterior Angles of a Triangle

**Conclusion 13.3.1** (Triangle Interior Angle Sum Theorem): The sum of the three interior angles of a triangle equals $180^\circ$.

**Conclusion 13.3.2** (Angles of a Right Triangle): The two acute angles of a right triangle are complementary; a triangle with two complementary angles is a right triangle.

**Definition 13.3.1** (Exterior Angle of a Triangle): The angle formed by one side of a triangle and the extension of another side is called an exterior angle of the triangle.

**Conclusion 13.3.3** (Properties of Exterior Angles of a Triangle): An exterior angle of a triangle equals the sum of the two interior angles not adjacent to it; an exterior angle of a triangle is greater than any interior angle not adjacent to it.

### Chapter 14 Congruent Triangles

#### 14.1 Congruent Triangles and Their Properties

**Definition 14.1.1** (Congruent Figures and Congruent Triangles): Two figures that can completely coincide are called congruent figures; two triangles that can completely coincide are called congruent triangles, denoted $\triangle ABC\cong\triangle A'B'C'$. The coinciding vertices, sides, and angles are respectively called corresponding vertices, corresponding sides, and corresponding angles.

**Conclusion 14.1.1** (Properties of Congruent Triangles): Corresponding sides of congruent triangles are equal, and corresponding angles are equal. Figures before and after translation, reflection, and rotation are congruent.

#### 14.2 Criteria for Congruence of Triangles

**Basic Fact 14.2.1** (SAS): Two triangles are congruent if two sides and their included angle are respectively equal.

**Basic Fact 14.2.2** (ASA): Two triangles are congruent if two angles and their included side are respectively equal.

**Conclusion 14.2.1** (AAS): Two triangles are congruent if two angles are respectively equal and the opposite side of one pair of equal angles is equal.

**Basic Fact 14.2.3** (SSS): Two triangles are congruent if the three sides are respectively equal.

**Conclusion 14.2.2** (HL): Two right triangles are congruent if the hypotenuse and one right-angled side are respectively equal.

#### 14.3 Angle Bisectors

**Conclusion 14.3.1** (Properties of Angle Bisectors): Points on the bisector of an angle are equidistant from the two sides of the angle.

**Conclusion 14.3.2** (Criterion for Angle Bisectors): A point inside the angle that is equidistant from the two sides of the angle lies on the angle bisector.

**Conclusion 14.3.3** (Angle Bisectors of a Triangle): The three angle bisectors of a triangle meet at one point, which is equidistant from the three sides.

### Chapter 15 Axial Symmetry

#### 15.1 Axial Symmetry of Figures

**Definition 15.1.1** (Axially Symmetric Figures and Figures in Axial Symmetry): A figure such that the parts on either side of a line can coincide after folding along that line is called an axially symmetric figure; the line is called the axis of symmetry, and the coinciding points are called symmetric points. If one figure folded along a line can coincide with another figure, the two figures are said to be axially symmetric about that line.

**Conclusion 15.1.1** (Properties of Axial Symmetry): Two figures in axial symmetry are congruent; the axis of symmetry perpendicularly bisects the line segment connecting symmetric points.

**Definition 15.1.2** (Perpendicular Bisector of a Line Segment): The line passing through the midpoint of a line segment and perpendicular to it is called the perpendicular bisector of that segment.

**Conclusion 15.1.2** (Properties of Perpendicular Bisectors): A point on the perpendicular bisector of a segment is equidistant from the two endpoints of the segment.

**Conclusion 15.1.3** (Criterion for Perpendicular Bisectors): A point equidistant from the two endpoints of a segment lies on the perpendicular bisector of that segment. The three perpendicular bisectors of a triangle meet at one point (the circumcenter), which is equidistant from the three vertices.

**Definition 15.1.3** (Converse Propositions): Two propositions whose hypotheses and conclusions are exactly reversed are converse propositions; if the converse of a theorem is proved true, the two are converse theorems.

#### 15.2 Drawing Axially Symmetric Figures

**Conclusion 15.2.1** (Drawing Axially Symmetric Figures): Draw the symmetric points of special points (endpoints, etc.) in the figure about the axis of symmetry, then connect them in sequence to obtain the symmetric figure. To make the symmetric point of a point: draw a perpendicular from the point to the axis, and on the perpendicular cut off a segment bisected by the foot.

**Conclusion 15.2.2** (Axial Symmetry in Coordinates): The point $\left(x,y\right)$ symmetric about the $x$-axis is $\left(x,-y\right)$; symmetric about the $y$-axis is $\left(-x,y\right)$; symmetric about the origin is $\left(-x,-y\right)$. The point symmetric about the line $x=a$ is $\left(2a-x,y\right)$; symmetric about the line $y=b$ is $\left(x,2b-y\right)$.

#### 15.3 Isosceles Triangles

**Definition 15.3.1** (Isosceles Triangles): A triangle with two equal sides is called an isosceles triangle; one with three equal sides is called an equilateral triangle.

**Conclusion 15.3.1** (Equal Sides Opposite Equal Angles): The two base angles of an isosceles triangle are equal.

**Conclusion 15.3.2** (The Three Lines Coincide): The median on the base, the altitude on the base, and the bisector of the vertex angle of an isosceles triangle coincide, and the line containing them is its axis of symmetry.

**Conclusion 15.3.3** (Equal Angles Opposite Equal Sides): A triangle with two equal angles is an isosceles triangle.

**Conclusion 15.3.4** (Equilateral Triangles): An equilateral triangle has all three angles equal, each $60^\circ$; a triangle with all three angles equal is equilateral; an isosceles triangle with one angle $60^\circ$ is equilateral.

**Conclusion 15.3.5** (Right Triangle with a $30^\circ$ Angle): In a right triangle, the right-angled side opposite the $30^\circ$ acute angle equals half the hypotenuse.

**Conclusion 15.3.6** (Inequality Relations Between Sides and Angles): In a triangle, the larger side is opposite the larger angle, and the larger angle is opposite the larger side.

### Chapter 16 Multiplication of Integral Expressions

#### 16.1 Operations on Powers

**Definition 16.1.1** (Multiplication of Powers with the Same Base): $a^m\cdot a^n=a^{m+n}$ ($m,n$ are both positive integers), that is, when multiplying powers with the same base, the base remains unchanged and the exponents are added. This property also holds for three or more powers with the same base.

**Definition 16.1.2** (Raising a Power to a Power): $\left(a^m\right)^n=a^{mn}$ ($m,n$ are both positive integers), that is, when raising a power to a power, the base remains unchanged and the exponents are multiplied.

**Definition 16.1.3** (Raising a Product to a Power): $\left(ab\right)^n=a^n b^n$ ($n$ is a positive integer), that is, raising a product to a power equals raising each factor of the product to that power and then multiplying the resulting powers. This property also holds for the product of three or more factors.

#### 16.2 Multiplication of Integral Expressions

**Definition 16.2.1** (Multiplying a Monomial by a Monomial): Multiply their coefficients and powers with the same base respectively as factors of the product; for a letter contained in only one monomial, take it together with its exponent as a factor of the product.

**Definition 16.2.2** (Multiplying a Monomial by a Polynomial): Multiply each term of the polynomial by the monomial, then add the resulting products, namely $p\left(a+b+c\right)=pa+pb+pc$.

**Definition 16.2.3** (Multiplying a Polynomial by a Polynomial): First multiply each term of one polynomial by each term of the other, then add the resulting products, namely $\left(a+b\right)\left(p+q\right)=ap+aq+bp+bq$.

**Definition 16.2.4** (Division of Powers with the Same Base): $a^m\div a^n=a^{m-n}$ ($a\ne0$, $m,n$ are both positive integers, $m> n$), that is, when dividing powers with the same base, the base remains unchanged and the exponents are subtracted.

**Definition 16.2.5** (Zero Exponent Power): $a^0=1$ ($a\ne0$), that is, the $0$-th power of any number not equal to $0$ equals $1$.

**Definition 16.2.6** (Dividing a Monomial by a Monomial): Divide the coefficients and powers with the same base respectively as factors of the quotient; for a letter contained only in the dividend, take it together with its exponent as a factor of the quotient.

**Definition 16.2.7** (Dividing a Polynomial by a Monomial): First divide each term of the polynomial by the monomial, then add the resulting quotients.

#### 16.3 Multiplication Formulas

**Definition 16.3.1** (Difference of Squares Formula): $\left(a+b\right)\left(a-b\right)=a^2-b^2$, that is, the product of the sum of two numbers and their difference equals the difference of their squares.

**Definition 16.3.2** (Perfect Square Formula): $\left(a+b\right)^2=a^2+2ab+b^2$, $\left(a-b\right)^2=a^2-2ab+b^2$, that is, the square of the sum (or difference) of two numbers equals the sum of their squares plus (or minus) $2$ times their product.

**Definition 16.3.3** (Rules for Adding Parentheses): If the sign before the parentheses is positive, the signs of all terms inside remain unchanged; if the sign before the parentheses is negative, the signs of all terms inside change. Namely $a+b+c=a+\left(b+c\right)$, $a-b-c=a-\left(b+c\right)$.

### Chapter 17 Factoring Polynomials

#### 17.1 Factoring by Extracting the Common Factor

**Definition 17.1.1** (Factoring): Transforming a polynomial into the product of several integral expressions is called factoring the polynomial. Factoring and multiplication of integral expressions are transformations in opposite directions.

**Definition 17.1.2** (Common Factor): A factor common to all terms of a polynomial is called the common factor of the terms.

**Definition 17.1.3** (Common Factor Extraction Method): If the terms of a polynomial have a common factor, one can extract that common factor and write the polynomial as the product of the common factor and another factor; this factoring method is called the common factor extraction method.

#### 17.2 Factoring Using Formulas

**Definition 17.2.1** (Difference of Squares Formula Method): $a^2-b^2=\left(a+b\right)\left(a-b\right)$, that is, the difference of the squares of two numbers equals the product of their sum and their difference.

**Definition 17.2.2** (Perfect Square Trinomial): Expressions such as $a^2+2ab+b^2$ and $a^2-2ab+b^2$ are called perfect square trinomials.

**Definition 17.2.3** (Perfect Square Formula Method): $a^2+2ab+b^2=\left(a+b\right)^2$, $a^2-2ab+b^2=\left(a-b\right)^2$.

**Definition 17.2.4** (Formula Method): Exchanging the two sides of the multiplication formulas gives formulas for factoring certain special polynomials; the method of using formulas to factor polynomials is called the formula method. Factoring should be carried on until every polynomial factor can no longer be factored.

### Chapter 18 Fractions

#### 18.1 Fractions and Their Basic Properties

**Definition 18.1.1** (Fractions): If $A,B$ represent two integral expressions, and $B$ contains a letter, then the expression $\frac{A}{B}$ is called a fraction. $A$ is called the numerator and $B$ the denominator. The fraction $\frac{A}{B}$ is meaningful only when $B\ne0$.

**Definition 18.1.2** (Basic Property of Fractions): Multiplying (or dividing) both the numerator and denominator of a fraction by the same integral expression not equal to $0$, the value of the fraction is unchanged, namely $\frac{A}{B}=\frac{A\cdot C}{B\cdot C}$, $\frac{A}{B}=\frac{A\div C}{B\div C}$ ($C\ne0$).

**Definition 18.1.3** (Reducing Fractions and Simplest Fractions): According to the basic property of fractions, canceling the common factor of the numerator and denominator of a fraction is called reducing the fraction. A fraction whose numerator and denominator have no common factor is called a simplest fraction.

**Definition 18.1.4** (Common Denominator and Least Common Denominator): According to the basic property of fractions, transforming several fractions with different denominators into fractions with the same denominator equal in value to the original fractions is called finding a common denominator. Generally, the product of the highest powers of all factors in the denominators is taken as the common denominator, called the least common denominator.

#### 18.2 Multiplication and Division of Fractions

**Definition 18.2.1** (Rules for Multiplication of Fractions): Multiplying a fraction by a fraction, the product of the numerators is taken as the numerator and the product of the denominators as the denominator, namely $\frac{a}{b}\cdot\frac{c}{d}=\frac{ac}{bd}$.

**Definition 18.2.2** (Rules for Division of Fractions): Dividing a fraction by a fraction, invert the numerator and denominator of the divisor and multiply by the dividend, namely $\frac{a}{b}\div\frac{c}{d}=\frac{a}{b}\cdot\frac{d}{c}=\frac{ad}{bc}$.

**Definition 18.2.3** (Raising a Fraction to a Power): When $n$ is a positive integer, $\left(\frac{a}{b}\right)^n=\frac{a^n}{b^n}$, that is, to raise a fraction to a power, raise the numerator and denominator to that power separately.

#### 18.3 Addition and Subtraction of Fractions

**Definition 18.3.1** (Addition and Subtraction of Fractions with the Same Denominator): Adding or subtracting fractions with the same denominator, the denominator remains unchanged and the numerators are added or subtracted, namely $\frac{a}{c}\pm\frac{b}{c}=\frac{a\pm b}{c}$.

**Definition 18.3.2** (Addition and Subtraction of Fractions with Different Denominators): Adding or subtracting fractions with different denominators, first find a common denominator to make them fractions with the same denominator, then add or subtract, namely $\frac{a}{b}\pm\frac{c}{d}=\frac{ad\pm bc}{bd}$.

#### 18.4 Integer Exponent Powers

**Definition 18.4.1** (Negative Integer Exponent Powers): When $n$ is a positive integer, $a^{-n}=\frac{1}{a^n}$ ($a\ne0$), that is, $a^{-n}$ is the reciprocal of $a^n$.

**Definition 18.4.2** (Properties of Operations on Integer Exponent Powers): (1) $a^m\cdot a^n=a^{m+n}$; (2) $\left(a^m\right)^n=a^{mn}$; (3) $\left(ab\right)^n=a^n b^n$ ($m,n$ are integers, the base is nonzero).

**Definition 18.4.3** (Scientific Notation for Positive Numbers Less Than 1): A positive number less than $1$ can be expressed in the form $a\times10^{-n}$, where $1\le a< 10$ and $n$ is a positive integer.

#### 18.5 Fractional Equations

**Definition 18.5.1** (Fractional Equation): An equation whose denominator contains the unknown is called a fractional equation.

**Definition 18.5.2** (Checking When Solving a Fractional Equation): When solving a fractional equation, the solution of the integral equation obtained after removing denominators may make a denominator in the original equation equal to $0$. Substitute the solution of the integral equation into the least common denominator; if its value is not $0$, the solution is a solution of the original fractional equation; otherwise, it is not.

### Chapter 19 Quadratic Radicals

### Chapter 21 Quadrilaterals

#### 21.1 Quadrilaterals and Polygons

**Definition 21.1.1** (Quadrilateral): In a plane, a closed figure formed by several line segments connected end to end in sequence is called a polygon; a closed figure formed by four line segments not on the same straight line, connected end to end in sequence, is called a quadrilateral.

**Conclusion 21.1.1** (Interior Angle Sum of a Quadrilateral): The interior angle sum of a quadrilateral equals \(360^{\circ}\).

**Conclusion 21.1.2** (Exterior Angle Sum of a Quadrilateral): The exterior angle sum of a quadrilateral equals \(360^{\circ}\).

#### 21.2 Parallelograms

**Definition 21.2.1** (Parallelogram): A quadrilateral with both pairs of opposite sides parallel is called a parallelogram, denoted \(\square ABCD\).

**Property 21.2.1** (Opposite Sides): Opposite sides of a parallelogram are equal.

**Property 21.2.2** (Opposite Angles): Opposite angles of a parallelogram are equal.

**Property 21.2.3** (Diagonals): The diagonals of a parallelogram bisect each other.

**Definition 21.2.2** (Distance Between Two Parallel Lines): Among two parallel lines, the distance from any point on one line to the other line is called the distance between the two parallel lines. Any two parallel line segments sandwiched between two parallel lines are equal.

**Criterion 21.2.1** (Opposite Sides): A quadrilateral with both pairs of opposite sides equal is a parallelogram.

**Criterion 21.2.2** (Opposite Angles): A quadrilateral with both pairs of opposite angles equal is a parallelogram.

**Criterion 21.2.3** (Diagonals): A quadrilateral whose diagonals bisect each other is a parallelogram.

**Criterion 21.2.4** (One Pair of Opposite Sides): A quadrilateral with one pair of opposite sides parallel and equal is a parallelogram.

**Definition 21.2.3** (Midsegment of a Triangle): The line segment connecting the midpoints of two sides of a triangle is called the midsegment of the triangle.

**Theorem 21.2.2** (Triangle Midsegment Theorem): The midsegment of a triangle is parallel to the third side and equals half of it.

#### 21.3 Special Parallelograms

**Definition 21.3.1** (Rectangle): A parallelogram with one right angle is called a rectangle.

**Property 21.3.1** (Angles of a Rectangle): All four angles of a rectangle are right angles.

**Property 21.3.2** (Diagonals of a Rectangle): The diagonals of a rectangle are equal. A rectangle is an axially symmetric figure.

**Conclusion 21.3.1** (Median to the Hypotenuse of a Right Triangle): The median to the hypotenuse of a right triangle equals half the hypotenuse.

**Criterion 21.3.1** (Diagonals): A parallelogram with equal diagonals is a rectangle.

**Criterion 21.3.2** (Three Right Angles): A quadrilateral with three right angles is a rectangle.

**Definition 21.3.2** (Rhombus): A parallelogram with one pair of adjacent sides equal is called a rhombus.

**Property 21.3.3** (Sides of a Rhombus): All four sides of a rhombus are equal.

**Property 21.3.4** (Diagonals of a Rhombus): The two diagonals of a rhombus are perpendicular to each other, and each bisects a pair of opposite angles. A rhombus is an axially symmetric figure. The area of a rhombus equals half the product of the lengths of its two diagonals.

**Criterion 21.3.3** (Diagonals): A parallelogram with perpendicular diagonals is a rhombus.

**Criterion 21.3.4** (Four Sides): A quadrilateral with four equal sides is a rhombus.

**Definition 21.3.3** (Square): A parallelogram with one pair of adjacent sides equal and one right angle is called a square. A square is both a rectangle with one pair of adjacent sides equal and a rhombus with one right angle.

**Property 21.3.5** (Square): A square has all the properties of a parallelogram, rectangle, and rhombus; its two diagonals divide the square into four congruent isosceles right triangles.

**Criterion 21.3.5** (Square): First determine it is a rectangle, then determine that this rectangle is also a rhombus; or first determine it is a rhombus, then determine that this rhombus is also a rectangle.

### Chapter 22 Functions

#### 22.1 Concept of a Function

**Definition 22.1.1** (Constants and Variables): In a changing process, a quantity whose value remains constant is a constant, and a quantity whose value changes is a variable.

**Definition 22.1.2** (Function): Generally, in a changing process, if there are two variables $x$ and $y$, and for each determined value of $x$, $y$ has a uniquely determined value corresponding to it, then we say $x$ is the independent variable and $y$ is a function of $x$. If when $x=a$, $y=b$, then $b$ is called the function value when the independent variable is $a$.

#### 22.2 Representation of Functions

**Definition 22.2.1** (Analytical Method): The method of representing a function relationship by a mathematical expression is called the analytical method.

**Definition 22.2.2** (Tabular Method): The method of listing in a table the corresponding values of the independent variable and the function is called the tabular method.

**Definition 22.2.3** (Graphical Method): The method of representing a function relationship by a graph is called the graphical method.

**Definition 22.2.4** (Drawing a Function Graph by Plotting Points): Generally, for a function, if each pair of corresponding values of the independent variable and the function is taken respectively as the abscissa and ordinate of a point, then the figure formed by these points in the coordinate plane is the graph of the function. The general steps of plotting a function graph are: list, plot points, connect.

### Chapter 23 Linear Functions

#### 23.1 Concept of a Linear Function

**Definition 23.1.1** (Direct Proportion Function): Generally, a function of the form $y=kx$ ($k$ is a constant, $k \neq 0$) is called a direct proportion function.

**Definition 23.1.2** (Linear Function): Generally, a function of the form $y=kx+b$ ($k$, $b$ are constants, $k \neq 0$) is called a linear function. When $b=0$, $y=kx$, so a direct proportion function is a special kind of linear function.

#### 23.2 Graph and Properties of a Linear Function

**Conclusion 23.2.1** (Graph of a Linear Function): The graph of the linear function $y=kx+b$ is a straight line, called the line $y=kx+b$. It can be obtained by translating the line $y=kx$ by $\left|b\right|$ units (upward when $b> 0$, downward when $b< 0$).

**Conclusion 23.2.2** (Properties of a Linear Function): When $k> 0$, the line $y=kx+b$ rises from left to right, and $y$ increases as $x$ increases; when $k< 0$, the line $y=kx+b$ falls from left to right, and $y$ decreases as $x$ increases.

#### 23.3 Linear Functions and Equations (Systems), Inequalities

**Conclusion 23.3.1** (Linear Functions and Linear Equations in One Unknown): The solution of the linear equation $kx+b=0$ is the abscissa of the intersection of the graph of $y=kx+b$ with the $x$-axis.

**Conclusion 23.3.2** (Linear Functions and Linear Inequalities in One Unknown): The solution set of the inequality $kx+b> 0$ is the range of the independent variable $x$ for which the value of $y=kx+b$ is greater than $0$; on the graph, it corresponds to the part of the line $y=kx+b$ above the $x$-axis.

**Conclusion 23.3.3** (Linear Functions and Systems of Linear Equations in Two Unknowns): The intersection coordinates of the graphs of two linear functions are the solution of the corresponding system of linear equations.

#### 23.4 Practical Problems and Linear Functions

**Conclusion 23.4.1** (Building a Linear Function Model): In practical problems, first determine the analytical expression of the function from the correspondence between variables, then use the graph and properties of the linear function to solve the problem.

### Chapter 24 Data Analysis

#### 24.1 Central Tendency of Data

**Definition 24.1.1** (Arithmetic Mean): Generally, for $n$ data $x_1, x_2, \ldots, x_n$, we call

$$\bar{x} = \frac{x_1 + x_2 + \cdots + x_n}{n}$$

the arithmetic mean of these $n$ data.

**Definition 24.1.2** (Weighted Mean): Generally, if the weights of the $n$ numbers $x_1, x_2, \ldots, x_n$ are respectively $w_1, w_2, \ldots, w_n$, then

$$\bar{x} = \frac{x_1 w_1 + x_2 w_2 + \cdots + x_n w_n}{w_1 + w_2 + \cdots + w_n}$$

is called the weighted mean of these $n$ numbers.

**Definition 24.1.3** (Median): Arrange a set of data in ascending (or descending) order; if the number of data is odd, the number in the middle position is called the median; if the number is even, the average of the two middle data is called the median.

**Definition 24.1.4** (Mode): The data that appears most frequently in a set is called the mode.

**Conclusion 24.1.5** (Characteristics of Mean, Median, and Mode): The mean can fully utilize all data information but is greatly affected by extreme values; the median is simple to compute and not easily affected by extreme values but cannot fully utilize all data information; the mode is not easily affected by extreme values but is not representative when the frequencies of the data do not differ much.

#### 24.2 Dispersion of Data

**Definition 24.2.1** (Deviation): For $n$ data $x_1, x_2, \ldots, x_n$, let $\bar{x}$ be their mean; $x_i - \bar{x}$ ($i=1, 2, \ldots, n$) is called the deviation of $x_i$ from the mean $\bar{x}$.

**Definition 24.2.2** (Sum of Squared Deviations):

$$\left(x_1 - \bar{x}\right)^2 + \left(x_2 - \bar{x}\right)^2 + \cdots + \left(x_n - \bar{x}\right)^2$$

is called the sum of squared deviations from the mean for these $n$ data, denoted $d^2$.

**Definition 24.2.3** (Variance): The mean of the squared deviations

$$s^2 = \frac{\left(x_1 - \bar{x}\right)^2 + \left(x_2 - \bar{x}\right)^2 + \cdots + \left(x_n - \bar{x}\right)^2}{n}$$

is called the variance of this set of data, denoted $s^2$. The larger the variance, the greater the dispersion; the smaller the variance, the smaller the dispersion.

#### 24.3 Quartiles of Data

**Definition 24.3.1** (Percentile): The value at each division point that divides the data into $100$ equal parts is called a percentile of this set.

**Definition 24.3.2** (Quartiles): The three division points that divide a set of data arranged in ascending order into four equal parts, from small to large, are respectively called the first quartile, second quartile (median), and third quartile, denoted $Q_1, Q_2, Q_3$.

**Definition 24.3.3** (Box Plot): A plot drawn using the five values of a data set — minimum, first quartile, median, third quartile, and maximum — is called a box plot.

**Definition 24.3.4** (Interquartile Range): The difference between the third quartile and the first quartile is called the interquartile range.

#### 24.4 Grouping of Data

**Definition 24.4.1** (Within-Group Sum of Squared Deviations): Dividing the data into two groups, the sum of the within-group sums of squared deviations is called the within-group sum of squared deviations.

**Definition 24.4.2** (Between-Group Sum of Squared Deviations): $d_{12}^2 = m\left(\bar{x}_1 - \bar{x}\right)^2 + \left(n-m\right)\left(\bar{x}_2 - \bar{x}\right)^2$ is called the between-group sum of squared deviations.

**Conclusion 24.4.3** (Grouping Principle): Group according to the principle of minimizing the within-group sum of squared deviations (equivalently, maximizing the between-group sum of squared deviations).

### Chapter 25 Quadratic Equations in One Unknown

#### 25.1 Concept of a Quadratic Equation in One Unknown

**Definition 25.1.1** (Quadratic Equation in One Unknown): An integral equation containing only one unknown, whose expressions are all integral, and whose highest degree of the unknown is $2$.

**Definition 25.1.2** (General Form): $ax^2+bx+c=0\ \left(a\ne0\right)$, where $ax^2$ is the quadratic term and $a$ the quadratic coefficient, $bx$ is the linear term and $b$ the linear coefficient, $c$ is the constant term; $a\ne0$ is stipulated.

**Definition 25.1.3** (Root): The value of the unknown that makes the left and right sides of a quadratic equation equal, i.e., the solution of the equation, also called the root.

#### 25.2 Reducing the Degree — Solving Quadratic Equations in One Unknown

**Conclusion 25.2.1** (Direct Taking of Square Roots): For the equation $x^2=p$, when $p> 0$ the two roots are $\pm\sqrt p$; when $p=0$ the two roots are equal to $0$; when $p< 0$ there are no real roots.

**Definition 25.2.1** (Completing the Square): Solving the equation by completing it into a perfect square; based on $a^2\pm2ab+b^2=\left(a\pm b\right)^2$, add the square of half the linear coefficient to both sides.

**Conclusion 25.2.2** (Reducing the Degree After Completing the Square): $\left(x+n\right)^2=p$; when $p> 0$, $x=-n\pm\sqrt p$; when $p=0$, $x=-n$; when $p< 0$, no real roots.

**Definition 25.2.2** (Discriminant): $\Delta=b^2-4ac$.

**Conclusion 25.2.3** (Determining the Nature of the Roots): $\Delta> 0$ two unequal real roots; $\Delta=0$ two equal real roots; $\Delta< 0$ no real roots.

**Definition 25.2.3** (Quadratic Formula): When $\Delta\ge0$, $x=\frac{-b\pm\sqrt{b^2-4ac}}{2a}$; substituting into the formula to find roots is called the formula method.

**Conclusion 25.2.4** (Product Is Zero): $ab=0\iff a=0$ or $b=0$.

**Definition 25.2.4** (Factoring Method): Factor the equation so that it becomes the product of two linear factors equal to $0$, then set each factor to $0$ to reduce the degree.

**Conclusion 25.2.5** (Relationship Between Roots and Coefficients / Vieta's Formulas): For $ax^2+bx+c=0\ \left(a\ne0\right)$, the two roots $x_1,x_2$ satisfy $x_1+x_2=-\frac ba$ and $x_1x_2=\frac ca$.

#### 25.3 Practical Problems and Quadratic Equations

**Conclusion 25.3.1** (Solving Word Problems by Setting Up Equations): Read the problem, set up the unknown, set up the equation, solve the equation, check the practical meaning of the root, and give the answer.

**Conclusion 25.3.2** (Growth Rate / Decline Rate): Original quantity $a$, average growth/decline rate $x$, after two periods it is $a\left(1\pm x\right)^2$.

**Conclusion 25.3.3** (Propagation Model): Initially $1$ person, each round infects $x$ people on average, after two rounds there are $\left(1+x\right)^2$ people.

**Conclusion 25.3.4** (Number of Matches): $n$ teams in a single round-robin total $\frac{n\left(n-1\right)}2$ matches; double round-robin total $n\left(n-1\right)$ matches.

**Conclusion 25.3.5** (Diagonals of a Convex Polygon): A convex $n$-gon has $\frac{n\left(n-3\right)}2$ diagonals.

### Chapter 26 Quadratic Functions

#### 26.1 Concept of a Quadratic Function

**Definition 26.1.1** (Quadratic Function): A function of the form $y=ax^2+bx+c$ ($a,b,c$ are constants, $a\ne0$); $a,b,c$ are respectively the quadratic coefficient, linear coefficient, and constant term.

#### 26.2 Graph and Properties of a Quadratic Function

**Conclusion 26.2.1** (Parabola $y=ax^2$): The axis of symmetry is the $y$-axis, the vertex is the origin; when $a> 0$ it opens upward with the vertex lowest, when $a< 0$ it opens downward with the vertex highest; the larger $\left|a\right|$ is, the narrower the opening; increasing/decreasing behavior depends on the sign of $a$.

**Conclusion 26.2.2** (Translation): Translating $y=ax^2$ vertically by $\left|k\right|$ gives $y=ax^2+k$, horizontally by $\left|h\right|$ gives $y=a\left(x-h\right)^2$, and combined gives $y=a\left(x-h\right)^2+k$.

**Conclusion 26.2.3** (Parabola $y=a\left(x-h\right)^2+k$): $a> 0$ opens upward, $a< 0$ opens downward; axis of symmetry $x=h$; vertex $\left(h,k\right)$; when $a> 0$, $x=h$ gives the minimum $k$, when $a< 0$ gives the maximum $k$.

**Conclusion 26.2.4** (Parabola $y=ax^2+bx+c$): Completing the square gives $y=a\left(x+\frac b{2a}\right)^2+\frac{4ac-b^2}{4a}$, axis of symmetry $x=-\frac b{2a}$, vertex $\left(-\frac b{2a},\frac{4ac-b^2}{4a}\right)$; $a> 0$ gives a minimum, $a< 0$ gives a maximum.

#### 26.3 Quadratic Functions and Quadratic Equations

**Conclusion 26.3.1** (Quadratic Functions and Quadratic Equations): The abscissas of the common points of the parabola $y=ax^2+bx+c$ with the $x$-axis are the real roots of the equation $ax^2+bx+c=0$; no common point, one common point, and two common points correspond respectively to no real roots, two equal real roots, and two unequal real roots.

### Chapter 28 Rotation

#### 28.1 Rotation of Figures

**Definition 28.1.1** (Rotation of a Figure): Rotating a plane figure around a point $O$ in the plane by an angle is called rotation of the figure; point $O$ is called the center of rotation, and the angle turned is called the angle of rotation.

**Definition 28.1.2** (Corresponding Points): If a point $P$ on a figure becomes point $P'$ after rotation, the two points are called corresponding points of the rotation.

**Property 28.1.1** (Properties of Rotation): The figures before and after rotation are congruent; corresponding points are equidistant from the center of rotation; the angles formed by the line segments connecting corresponding points to the center of rotation are equal, all equal to the angle of rotation.

#### 28.2 Central Symmetry

**Definition 28.2.1** (Central Symmetry): Rotating a figure around a point by $180^\circ$, if the rotated figure can coincide with another figure, the two figures are said to be centrally symmetric about that point; this point is called the center of symmetry, and the coinciding points are called symmetric points.

**Property 28.2.1** (Properties of Central Symmetry): For two figures in central symmetry, the line segments connecting symmetric points pass through the center of symmetry and are bisected by it; the two figures are congruent.

**Definition 28.2.2** (Centrally Symmetric Figure): Rotating a figure around a point by $180^\circ$, if the rotated figure can coincide with the original figure, this figure is called a centrally symmetric figure, and this point is its center of symmetry.

**Property 28.2.2** (Coordinates of Points Symmetric About the Origin): When two points are symmetric about the origin, their coordinate signs are opposite; the point symmetric to $P\left(x,y\right)$ about the origin is $P'\left(-x,-y\right)$.

### Chapter 29 Circles

#### 29.1 Concepts Related to Circles

**Definition 29.1.1** (Circle): In a plane, the figure formed by rotating the line segment $OP$ around its fixed endpoint $O$ for one full turn, with the other endpoint $P$ tracing, is called a circle; $O$ is called the center and $OP$ the radius.

**Property 29.1.1** (Positional Relationship Between a Point and a Circle): Let the radius be $r$ and the distance from the point to the center be $d$; then $d> r$ outside the circle, $d=r$ on the circle, $d< r$ inside the circle.

**Definition 29.1.2** (Chords, Diameters, Arcs): The line segment connecting two points on a circle is called a chord; a chord passing through the center is called a diameter; the part between two points on a circle is called an arc; an arc larger than a semicircle is a major arc, and one smaller than a semicircle is a minor arc.

**Theorem 29.1.1** (Three Points Determine a Circle): Three points not on a straight line determine a circle; the circle passing through the three vertices of a triangle is called the circumcircle, and its center the circumcenter.

**Method 29.1.1** (Proof by Contradiction): First make an assumption opposite to the conclusion, then derive a contradiction, thereby proving the original proposition.

#### 29.2 Properties Related to Circles

**Property 29.2.1** (Axial Symmetry of a Circle): A circle is an axially symmetric figure, and the line containing any diameter is its axis of symmetry.

**Theorem 29.2.1** (Perpendicular Chord Theorem): A diameter perpendicular to a chord bisects the chord and bisects the two arcs subtended by the chord.

**Corollary 29.2.1** (Corollary to the Perpendicular Chord Theorem): A diameter bisecting a chord (that is not a diameter) is perpendicular to the chord and bisects the two arcs subtended by the chord.

**Definition 29.2.1** (Central Angle): An angle whose vertex is at the center is called a central angle.

**Property 29.2.2** (Rotational Symmetry of a Circle): A circle is a centrally symmetric figure with the center as the center of symmetry; a circle coincides with itself after rotation by any angle around the center.

**Theorem 29.2.2** (Relationship Among Central Angles, Arcs, and Chords): In the same circle or equal circles, equal central angles subtend equal arcs and equal chords; and conversely.

**Definition 29.2.2** (Inscribed Angle): An angle whose vertex is on the circle and whose both sides intersect the circle is called an inscribed angle.

**Theorem 29.2.3** (Inscribed Angle Theorem): The inscribed angle subtended by an arc equals half the central angle subtended by the same arc.

**Corollary 29.2.2** (Corollary to the Inscribed Angle Theorem): Inscribed angles subtended by the same arc or equal arcs are equal; the inscribed angle subtended by a semicircle (or diameter) is a right angle; the chord subtended by a $90^\circ$ inscribed angle is a diameter.

**Definition 29.2.3** (Cyclic Polygon): A polygon whose all vertices lie on the same circle is called a cyclic polygon, and the circle is called its circumcircle.

**Property 29.2.3** (Properties of Cyclic Quadrilaterals): Opposite angles of a cyclic quadrilateral are supplementary.

#### 29.3 Arc Length and Sector Area

**Formula 29.3.1** (Arc Length Formula): In a circle of radius $R$, the arc length subtended by a central angle of $n^\circ$ is $l=\frac{n\pi R}{180}$.

**Definition 29.3.1** (Sector): The figure bounded by the two radii forming a central angle and the arc subtended by it is called a sector.

**Formula 29.3.2** (Sector Area Formula): $S=\frac{n\pi R^2}{360}=\frac12 lR$.

**Definition 29.3.2** (Generatrix of a Cone): The line segment connecting the vertex of a cone to any point on the base circumference is called the generatrix.

**Formula 29.3.3** (Lateral Area of a Cone): With generatrix $l$ and base radius $r$, the lateral area is $\pi rl$, and the total area is $\pi r\left(r+l\right)$.

### Chapter 30 Positional Relationship Between a Line and a Circle

#### 30.1 Line and Circle

**Definition 30.1.1** (Positional Relationship Between a Line and a Circle): No common point is called separate; one common point is called tangent (the line is called the tangent, the common point the point of tangency); two common points is called intersecting.

**Property 30.1.1** (Criterion for Positional Relationship): Comparing the distance $d$ from center to line with the radius $r$: $d> r$ separate, $d=r$ tangent, $d< r$ intersecting.

**Theorem 30.1.1** (Properties of a Tangent Line): The tangent to a circle is perpendicular to the radius at the point of tangency.

**Theorem 30.1.2** (Criterion for a Tangent Line): A line passing through the outer endpoint of a radius and perpendicular to that radius is a tangent to the circle.

**Definition 30.1.2** (Length of a Tangent Segment): On a tangent from a point outside the circle, the length of the segment between that point and the point of tangency is called the tangent length.

**Theorem 30.1.3** (Tangent Length Theorem): From a point outside a circle, the two tangents to the circle have equal length, and the line connecting the point to the center bisects the angle between the two tangents.

#### 30.2 Incircle of a Triangle

**Definition 30.2.1** (Incircle of a Triangle): The circle tangent to all three sides of a triangle is called the incircle, and its center the incenter (intersection of the three angle bisectors).

#### 30.3 Regular Polygons and Circles

**Theorem 30.3.1** (Regular Polygons and Circles): Dividing a circle into $n$ equal parts and connecting the division points in sequence gives a regular $n$-gon inscribed in the circle.

**Property 30.3.1** (Properties of Regular Polygons): The center of the circumcircle of a regular polygon is called the center, the circumradius the radius, the inradius the apothem, and the central angle subtended by each side the central angle; the central angle of a regular $n$-gon is $\frac{360^\circ}{n}$.

### Chapter 27 Similarity

#### 27.1 Similarity of Figures

**Definition 27.1.1** (Similar Figures): Figures with the same shape are called similar figures; if two figures are similar, one can be regarded as obtained by enlarging or reducing the other.

**Definition 27.1.2** (Proportional Line Segments): For four line segments $a,b,c,d$, if the ratio of two of them equals the ratio of the other two, i.e., $\frac{a}{b}=\frac{c}{d}$ (i.e., $ad=bc$), the four segments are said to be proportional.

**Definition 27.1.3** (Similar Polygons): Two polygons with the same number of sides, if their angles are respectively equal and their sides proportional, are called similar polygons; the ratio of corresponding sides is called the similarity ratio. Corresponding angles of similar polygons are equal and corresponding sides are proportional.

#### 27.2 Similar Triangles

**Definition 27.2.1** (Similar Triangles): In $\triangle ABC$ and $\triangle A'B'C'$, if $\angle A=\angle A'$, $\angle B=\angle B'$, $\angle C=\angle C'$, and $\frac{AB}{A'B'}=\frac{BC}{B'C'}=\frac{AC}{A'C'}=k$, then $\triangle ABC\backsim\triangle A'B'C'$, where $k$ is the similarity ratio.

**Basic Fact 27.2.1** (Parallel Lines Divide Segments Proportionally): Two straight lines cut by a set of parallel lines give proportional corresponding segments.

**Theorem 27.2.1** (Preliminary Theorem): A line parallel to one side of a triangle cuts the other two sides (or their extensions), and the corresponding segments are proportional; a line parallel to one side intersecting the other two sides forms a triangle similar to the original.

**Criterion Theorem 27.2.2** (SSS): Two triangles are similar if their three sides are proportional.

**Criterion Theorem 27.2.3** (SAS): Two triangles are similar if two sides are proportional and the included angle is equal.

**Criterion Theorem 27.2.4** (AA): Two triangles are similar if two angles are respectively equal.

**Criterion Theorem 27.2.5** (HL): Two right triangles are similar if their hypotenuses and one right-angled side are proportional.

**Property 27.2.1** (Corresponding Segments): The ratios of corresponding altitudes, corresponding medians, and corresponding angle bisectors of similar triangles all equal the similarity ratio; the ratio of corresponding segments equals the similarity ratio.

**Property 27.2.2** (Area Ratio): The ratio of the areas of similar triangles equals the square of the similarity ratio.

#### 27.3 Homothety

**Definition 27.3.1** (Homothetic Figures): If the points $A,B,\ldots,P,\ldots$ on one figure correspond respectively to the points $A',B',\ldots,P',\ldots$ on another, and the connecting lines $AA',BB',\ldots,PP',\ldots$ all pass through the same point $O$, and $\frac{OA'}{OA}=\frac{OB'}{OB}=\cdots=\frac{OP'}{OP}=\cdots$, then the two figures are homothetic, and $O$ is the center of homothety. Homothetic figures are special similar figures with corresponding sides parallel.

**Definition 27.3.2** (Coordinate Rule for Homothety): In a Cartesian coordinate system, if the origin is taken as the center of homothety and a figure homothetic to the original is drawn with similarity ratio $k$, then the point on the homothetic figure corresponding to $\left(x,y\right)$ on the original has coordinates $\left(kx,ky\right)$ or $\left(-kx,-ky\right)$.

### Chapter 28 Trigonometric Functions of Acute Angles
